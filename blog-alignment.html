Aligning an optical setup: from a laser on a table to a working instrument

Every instrument in this reading path works only once its light goes where the design says it should. This tutorial is the practical part: laser safety and what the classes mean, beam height and the table grid, the tools on an alignment bench, walking a beam with two mirrors and two irises (and why one pairing converges and the other runs away), placing lenses, collimating, cleaning a beam with a spatial filter, coupling into a single-mode fibre, getting fringes in an interferometer, matching light into a slit or an objective, and keeping it all aligned when the room warms up.

Parts 2, 3 and 4 described instruments as finished designs. In the lab they start as a laser, a breadboard and a box of mounts, and the gap between the two is alignment. It is often taught by watching someone else, and much of it can be explained with the optics of Part 1: straight lines, small angles, Gaussian beams and two-beam interference. Every interactive figure here is calculated from the equations in the text. Numbers in square brackets refer to the references.

01What “aligned” means

An aligned optical system has four properties (Figure 1). The beam travels along a defined axis: parallel to the table and to the rows of holes, at one height everywhere. It passes through the centre of every lens, aperture and mirror. It meets lenses and flat windows at normal incidence, unless the design asks for an angle. And each element sits at the right distance along the axis, so that the beam is collimated, focused or imaged where it should be.

The first two are the job of the steering mirrors; the third is the job of the mounts; the fourth is the job of the translation stages and a ruler. A useful habit is to align in that order, and never to correct an error in one with another: a lens moved sideways to bring a decentred beam back to a target hides a tilt that will reappear the moment anything else changes.

02Laser safety first

Alignment is the most dangerous thing done with a laser. The beam is open, mounts are being moved, and stray reflections go in directions nobody planned. The eye focuses a collimated beam to a spot a few micrometres across on the retina, multiplying the irradiance by about 10⁵, so a beam that is harmless on the skin can burn the retina in less than a blink [1, 3].

Lasers are sorted into classes by the international standard IEC 60825-1 [1] (and, in the USA, the closely aligned ANSI Z136.1 [2]) according to the accessible emission. For a visible, continuous beam the approximate limits are:

ClassVisible CW powerWhat it means
1≤ 0.39 mWsafe under all reasonable conditions of use
2≤ 1 mWthe blink reflex (≈ 0.25 s) protects against an accidental glance; do not stare
3R≤ 5 mWlow risk, but direct viewing can exceed the exposure limit
3B≤ 500 mWdirect beam and specular reflections are hazardous; diffuse reflections usually not
4> 500 mWdirect, specular and diffuse reflections are hazardous; skin and fire hazard

These limits come from the maximum permissible exposure (MPE). For visible light and exposures between 10 µs and 10 s the retinal MPE is \(H = 18\,t^{0.75}\) J m⁻², which for the 0.25 s blink is \(25.5\ \text{W m}^{-2} = 2.55\ \text{mW cm}^{-2}\); through a 7 mm dark-adapted pupil that is about 1 mW — the class 2 limit [1, 2]. Protective eyewear is rated by its optical density at the laser wavelength, \(\text{OD} = \log_{10}(1/T)\), and must bring the power that can enter the eye below that level:

$$\text{OD} \ \ge\ \log_{10}\frac{P_\text{eye}}{P_\text{MPE}} .$$

Figure 2 applies this to a visible continuous beam. It is a teaching illustration: the standards include many corrections (wavelength, pulse duration, extended sources, repeated pulses), and the eyewear for a real laser is chosen by the laser-safety officer from the standard and the manufacturer's data, not from a figure.

Rules that prevent most accidents

Keep the beam horizontal and well below eye level; never bend down to beam height. Take off watches and rings. Align at the lowest power that you can see — attenuate with a neutral-density filter or run the laser just above threshold. Block every beam that leaves the useful path with a beam dump, and know where each reflection from a lens, window or polariser goes. Put the eyewear on before the shutter opens, and use cards and viewers instead of your eyes for invisible beams.

03Beam height and the table grid

An optical table is a ruler. Metric tables have M6 tapped holes on a 25 mm grid; imperial tables have ¼-20 holes on a 1 inch grid. The rows of holes are the reference lines: a beam that runs along a row is straight, and a mirror at 45° to the rows folds it by exactly 90° onto another row.

Choose one beam height before anything else — often 75 to 125 mm above the table, set by the tallest mount in the design — and keep it everywhere. Most lasers do not emit at that height or exactly parallel to the table, so the first two mirrors after the laser are a periscope or a pair of steering mirrors whose only job is to put the beam on the chosen row at the chosen height. Everything afterwards then mounts on posts of a known length, and a part can be swapped without realigning the rest.

Mark the line once

Put two irises (or two height targets) on the reference row, one near and one as far away as the table allows, both at the beam height. They define the axis for the rest of the build, and you can come back to them whenever something has drifted.

04The alignment toolbox

ToolWhat it tells you
Irises on postswhether the beam passes a point on the axis; closing them gives a sharp, centred reference
Alignment cards, ruler, height gaugebeam height and position; a card with a hole lets you see a back-reflection around the beam
IR viewer, phosphor or upconversion cardswhere an infrared beam is — a silicon camera without its IR filter also sees up to about 1.1 µm
Camera or beam profilerthe beam centre to a few micrometres, its size and shape; use filters, never the open beam
Power metertransmission through a pinhole or fibre, and losses along the path
Shear platecollimation, from the direction of its interference fringes
Retro-reflectionwhether a surface is perpendicular: its reflection comes back along the incoming beam

Two pieces of hardware make the rest possible. Kinematic mounts hold an optic on three contact points — a cone, a groove and a flat — so that two screws tilt it about two independent axes without play [10]. And translation stages move a lens, pinhole or fibre along one axis with a micrometer screw.

05Two mirrors, four knobs: walking the beam

A beam in a plane has two degrees of freedom: where it is and which way it points. Out of the plane there are two more. One mirror with two knobs can fix only the direction (or only the position at one point), so putting a beam on a defined line always takes two mirrors and their four knobs, and two points to define the line: two irises.

Unfold the path so the beam runs along one axis \(z\) (Figure 3). Tilting a mirror at \(z_m\) by \(\delta\) turns the reflected beam by \(2\delta\), so the beam height at a later point \(z\) changes by \(2\delta\,(z-z_m)\). With mirrors at \(z_1 < z_2\) and irises at \(z_3 < z_4\), the standard procedure is:

  1. adjust M1 to put the beam through the near iris;
  2. adjust M2 to put the beam through the far iris;
  3. repeat until both are centred.

Each round multiplies the error by a fixed factor that depends only on the geometry:

$$\rho = \frac{(z_3-z_2)\,(z_4-z_1)}{(z_3-z_1)\,(z_4-z_2)} \ <\ 1 .$$

Swap the pairing — M2 on the near iris, M1 on the far one — and the factor becomes \(1/\rho > 1\): every round makes it worse. This is why “first mirror, first iris” is the rule. The factor is small, and the walking fast, when the irises are far from each other and the near iris is close to M2: then M1 mostly moves the beam at the near iris and M2 mostly turns it at the far one. Try it in Figure 3: press “One round of walking”, then switch the rule.

Two refinements save time. Overshoot on purpose: if adjusting M2 makes the beam miss the near iris by a millimetre, go past the centre of the far iris by the factor the geometry suggests, and the next correction is smaller. And when the knobs run out of travel, the error is usually upstream: move the post rather than the knob.

06A level beam and a straight line

The same procedure works out of the plane: the vertical knobs of M1 and M2, walked against the height of the two irises, make the beam level at the chosen height. Check by sliding one iris (or a height card on a post) along the row: the beam must stay centred all the way. A beam that rises 1 mm over 1 m is tilted by 1 mrad, and three folds later it has left the optics.

Once the axis is set, put the irises at the beginning and end of each straight section, and align every later part to the irises, not to the previous part. Errors then stay local instead of adding up along the path.

07Putting in a lens

A lens has two jobs to be aligned for: it must be centred on the beam and perpendicular to it.

Centring. A thin lens displaced sideways by \(d\) acts like a centred lens plus a prism: it deflects the beam by \(d/f\) radians. With the far iris (or a mark on the wall) in place before the lens goes in, move the lens sideways until the beam lands on the same spot again. The shorter the focal length, the more sensitive this is: 0.5 mm off a 25 mm lens is 20 mrad, 4 cm at 2 m.

Tilt. Each lens surface reflects a few per cent of the light. If the lens is tilted by \(\tau\), that weak back-reflection returns at \(2\tau\) to the incoming beam; at a distance \(L\) it lands \(2\tau L\) from it. Look for it on the back of the first iris (a card with a hole works too) and tilt the lens until the reflection goes straight back through the hole. A tilted lens adds astigmatism and coma, which a camera sees as a smeared focus.

For several lenses, insert them from the end of the path backwards, or insert them all, then centre each one with the others removed. Put spherical surfaces the right way round: a plano-convex lens focusing a collimated beam has its curved side towards the collimated beam, which roughly halves its spherical aberration [4].

08Collimating a beam

A beam expander (two lenses \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) a distance \(f_1+f_2\) apart) makes the beam \(M = f_2/f_1\) times wider and \(M\) times less divergent. It is collimated only at the right spacing; a spacing error \(\Delta\) leaves a weakly focused or weakly diverging beam. The Gaussian-beam \(q\) parameter, propagated through each lens with \(1/q' = 1/q - 1/f\) [5, 6], gives the beam radius \(w(z)\) everywhere (Figure 5). A spacing error of a fraction of a millimetre is invisible at the lens but obvious 10 m later.

How to check it in practice:

  • Far-field test. Measure the beam size near the lens and as far away as possible (a corridor, or a long fold path); adjust the spacing until they agree.
  • Shear plate. A thick, slightly wedged glass plate at 45° reflects two copies of the beam from its two faces, sheared sideways. Their interference fringes run parallel to a reference line only when the wavefront is flat; a curved wavefront rotates them one way or the other [7, 8].
  • Autocollimation. Send the beam onto a flat mirror and back through the lens; when the source sits in the focal plane, the returning beam focuses back onto it.

09Cleaning a beam: the spatial filter

Dust, scratches and diffraction from apertures put ripples on a laser beam. A lens focuses the beam, and in its focal plane the clean Gaussian part becomes a small spot while each ripple of period \(p\) becomes a pair of points a distance \(\lambda f/p\) off axis — the lens is doing a Fourier transform [9]. A pinhole that passes the spot and blocks the rest cleans the beam.

For a beam of diameter \(D\) (1/e²) the focused spot has radius

$$w_0 = \frac{\lambda f}{\pi\,D/2}, \qquad \text{diameter } 2w_0 = \frac{4\lambda f}{\pi D},$$

and a pinhole of radius \(a\) passes the fraction \(T = 1-\exp(-2a^2/w_0^2)\). The usual choice is a pinhole about 1.5 times the spot diameter: it passes about 99 % of the Gaussian and blocks ripples of all but the longest periods (Figure 6).

Aligning one is a lesson in patience. Start with the pinhole removed and centre the lens. Put the pinhole in, far out of focus, and move it sideways (x and y) until the transmitted light is brightest on a card. Then move it towards focus in small steps, re-centring x and y at each step; the transmitted pattern turns from rings into a clean, round spot when you are close. Finish on a power meter.

10Coupling into a single-mode fibre

A single-mode fibre accepts only light that matches its one guided mode, which is close to a Gaussian with a mode field diameter (MFD) of a few micrometres. The fraction coupled is the overlap integral of the focused beam with that mode. For two Gaussians of radii \(w_1\) (the focused beam) and \(w_2\) (the mode), with a lateral offset \(x\), a focus error \(z\) and an angle \(\theta\) [11, 12]:

$$\eta = \frac{4}{\left(\dfrac{w_1}{w_2}+\dfrac{w_2}{w_1}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{\lambda z}{\pi w_1 w_2}\right)^2} \;\exp\!\left(-\frac{2x^2}{w_1^2+w_2^2}\right) \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\,(\pi w_1 w_2\theta/\lambda)^2}{w_1^2+w_2^2}\right).$$

The first factor says choose the lens so that the focused spot equals the MFD, \(2\lambda f/(\pi D/2)\approx\) MFD; the second says that a lateral error of about a micrometre already costs a decibel (Figure 7). At 1550 nm in a standard telecom fibre (MFD 10.4 µm [13]) the tolerances are about twice as loose as at 633 nm.

The practical procedure: first send light backwards out of the fibre if you can (a fibre-coupled laser or a visible fault locator) and use it to centre the coupling lens and the two steering mirrors on the output of the fibre. Then couple forward, find any transmitted light on a power meter (in a multimode fibre first, if necessary), and walk the two mirrors exactly as in Figure 3 — near mirror for position, far mirror for angle — while adjusting the lens focus. Expect 70–85 % for a good, mode-matched setup.

11Aligning an interferometer

In a Michelson or Mach–Zehnder interferometer two beams must overlap in position and direction to better than a fraction of a fringe. If the two returning beams cross at a small angle \(2\alpha\) (one mirror tilted by \(\alpha\)), straight fringes appear with spacing \(\lambda/(2\alpha)\); if their wavefront curvatures differ, the fringes are circles; and if the path difference exceeds the coherence length \(L_c\) of the source, the fringes wash out [14].

The procedure follows the figure. Overlap the two spots on a card far away (the far field fixes the angle) and at the beam splitter output (the near field fixes the position); fringes appear once the angle is below about \(\lambda/\)(beam size). Then reduce the tilt until the fringes widen and “fluff out”. With a short-coherence source (an LED, white light), align first with a laser, then scan one arm slowly until the coloured fringes of zero path difference appear — they exist only within a few micrometres of it.

12Into a slit or an objective

Two common end points come from the earlier parts of this path.

A spectrometer slit (Part 2). Light focused onto the slit should fill the grating but not overfill it: match the f-number of the input lens to that of the spectrometer, \(N = f/D\). Light at a faster cone than the spectrometer accepts is lost as stray light inside; a slower cone wastes resolution. Image the source onto the slit, centre it with the slit opened wide, then close the slit to the working width while watching the signal.

A microscope objective (Part 3). For a laser focus (confocal, Raman, tweezers) the beam must enter along the objective's axis and fill its back aperture. Use two irises on the axis of the objective turret (an empty position screwed into a target helps), walk the beam onto them with two mirrors, and expand it so that its 1/e² diameter is about the size of the back aperture. A beam that enters off axis gives a tilted, aberrated focus; one that is too small gives a larger spot than the numerical aperture promises.

13Keeping it aligned: drift and stability

A setup that was perfect in the morning can be lost by the afternoon. The usual cause is temperature: a post of height \(h\) and expansion coefficient \(\alpha\) grows by \(\Delta h = \alpha\,h\,\Delta T\) [15]. If every post warms equally, the whole beam rises together and little changes. Trouble starts when one mount moves relative to the beam: a lens of focal length \(f\) whose post grows by \(\Delta h\) is decentred by that amount and steers the beam by \(\Delta h/f\) (Section 07), and a fibre or pinhole on a warming post simply moves off the focus. One kelvin on a 100 mm steel post is 1.7 µm — already more than the lateral tolerance of a single-mode fibre (Figure 9).

  • Short and stiff. Keep the beam low and the posts short and thick; use pedestal posts with clamping forks rather than tall thin posts.
  • Same material, same temperature. Matched posts that warm together move together; keep heat sources (lamps, electronics, the laser power supply) off the table and cover the setup against air currents.
  • Let it settle. Mounts relax for hours after being tightened; lasers drift while they warm up. Align after the laser has run for its warm-up time and re-check the next day.
  • Lock what is finished. Tighten locking nuts on adjusters that should not move, and leave the irises in place as references.

14A recipe from start to finish

  1. Plan. Draw the setup to scale on the table grid — on paper, or in a ray-tracing layout tool such as RNBeamX — with beam height, every element, its mount and every stray reflection.
  2. Safety. Eyewear, beam dumps, curtains, signs, lowest workable power.
  3. Laser and axis. Mount the laser; with two mirrors (or a periscope) bring the beam onto a reference row at the beam height; set two irises; walk the beam until it passes both.
  4. Folds. For every fold, place the mirror on the row intersection, set two irises on the new row, walk with the last two mirrors.
  5. Apertures before lenses. Put in irises, polarisers, filters and windows; tilt flat optics slightly so their reflections go into dumps, not back into the laser.
  6. Lenses, last to first. Centre each lens on the irises; set its tilt by back-reflection; set its position along the axis by collimation or focus.
  7. End points. Pinhole, fibre, slit, objective or detector, each on its own stages.
  8. Measure. Power at each stage, beam profile at the output, a short drift test.
  9. Record. Photograph the setup and note heights, distances and settings; mark the positions of key mounts on the table with tape.

The beam moves when a lens is insertedthe lens is decentred; move it sideways until the far spot returns
A focused spot has a tail or a crosslens tilted (coma, astigmatism), or used the wrong way round; check the back-reflection
Fringes or rings in a beam profiledust or an etalon between parallel surfaces; clean, or tilt windows and filters slightly
