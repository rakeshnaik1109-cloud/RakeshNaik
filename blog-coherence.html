Interferometry and coherence: measuring with the wave nature of light

Two beams of light can add up to darkness — and that simple fact measures lengths to a fraction of an atom, tests telescope mirrors, sees through the layers of the retina and hears black holes merge a billion light years away. This tutorial explains interference from the start: when two beams interfere and when they do not, what temporal and spatial coherence are and how the spectrum and the size of a source set them, the family of interferometers, counting fringes, testing surfaces, white-light interferometry and optical coherence tomography, the quantum limit of a measurement, photon bunching and speckle.

This part explains why fringes appear at all, what decides how clear they are, and how that is put to work. Every interactive figure is calculated from the equations in the text. 01Two waves, one pattern

Detectors respond to intensity, the time average of the squared field, not to the field itself, which oscillates about 5 × 10¹⁴ times a second for visible light. When two fields \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) overlap, the intensity is not simply \(I_1 + I_2\):

$$I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1 I_2}\;|\gamma_{12}|\cos\theta\,\cos\varphi ,$$

where \(\varphi\) is the phase difference between the two beams, \(\theta\) the angle between their polarisations and \(|\gamma_{12}|\), between 0 and 1, the degree of coherence: how well the phase of one beam stays in step with the other during the measurement [1, 2]. The third term is the interference. As the path difference changes, \(\cos\varphi\) swings between +1 and −1 and the intensity between a maximum and a minimum, and the clarity of the fringes is measured by their visibility

$$V = \frac{I_\text{max}-I_\text{min}}{I_\text{max}+I_\text{min}} = \frac{2\sqrt{I_1I_2}}{I_1+I_2}\,|\gamma_{12}|\cos\theta .$$

Three things can spoil the fringes (Figure 1): unequal intensities, orthogonal polarisations, and a lack of coherence. The average intensity is always \(I_1+I_2\): interference moves energy from the dark fringes to the bright ones; it does not create or destroy it.

02Young's experiment

In the first years of the nineteenth century Thomas Young split a narrow beam of sunlight, admitted through a small hole, into two and saw fringes where the two parts overlapped [3] — the experiment, now usually done with two slits, that established the wave theory of light. Two slits a distance \(d\) apart, seen from a screen at distance \(L\), produce fringes with spacing

$$\Delta x = \frac{\lambda L}{d},$$

under an envelope set by the diffraction of each slit of width \(a\), whose first zeros sit at \(x = \pm\lambda L/a\). The pinhole in front was essential. A source of width \(s\) at distance \(R\) before the slits is a collection of independent points, each producing its own fringe pattern, shifted by an amount that depends on where the point is. Their sum keeps a visibility of [2, 5]

$$|\gamma_{12}| = \left|\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi s d}{\lambda R}\right)\right| ,$$

which falls to zero when \(d = \lambda R/s\). Figure 2 shows all three effects; with white light, each colour has its own fringe spacing, and only the central few fringes, where all colours still agree, remain visible.

03What coherence measures

Real light is never a perfect sine wave. Its phase wanders on a time scale set by its spectrum, and different points across the beam wander differently when the source is extended. Coherence theory describes this with the mutual coherence function, the correlation between the field at two points and two times [2, 4, 5]:

$$\Gamma_{12}(\tau) = \langle E^*(\mathbf r_1, t)\,E(\mathbf r_2, t+\tau)\rangle, \qquad \gamma_{12}(\tau) = \frac{\Gamma_{12}(\tau)}{\sqrt{I_1 I_2}} .$$

Its magnitude is exactly the \(|\gamma_{12}|\) in the visibility above. Two special cases carry most of the practical meaning:

  • Temporal coherence, \(\gamma(\tau)\) at one point: how long the wave remembers its phase. It decides how large a path difference an interferometer can tolerate (a Michelson splits one beam and delays one half).
  • Spatial coherence, \(\gamma_{12}(0)\) between two points: how far apart two points across the beam can be and still interfere (Young's two slits sample the beam at two points).

04Temporal coherence and the spectrum

The Wiener–Khinchin theorem links the temporal coherence to the spectrum: \(\gamma(\tau)\) is the Fourier transform of the normalised power spectrum \(S(\nu)\) [6, 7, 5]:

$$\gamma(\tau) = \int_0^\infty S(\nu)\, e^{-2\pi i \nu \tau}\, d\nu .$$

A narrow spectrum gives a long-lasting \(\gamma\); a broad one, a short one. For a line of width \(\Delta\nu\) (or \(\Delta\lambda\) in wavelength) the coherence time and length are of order

$$\tau_c \approx \frac{1}{\Delta\nu}, \qquad L_c = c\,\tau_c \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda},$$

with factors close to 1 that depend on the line shape and the definition used. A Gaussian line gives a Gaussian \(|\gamma|\), a Lorentzian line an exponential one, and a doublet gives fringes that fade and return — the sodium D lines, 0.6 nm apart [8], beat with a period of 0.29 mm of mirror travel (Figure 3). This is the principle of the Fourier-transform spectrometer of Part 2, run in reverse: there the interferogram is measured and the spectrum computed from it.

05Spatial coherence and the size of the source

The van Cittert–Zernike theorem [9, 10] states that the spatial coherence produced by an incoherent source is the Fourier transform of its intensity distribution — the same mathematics as diffraction, applied to the source. For a uniform disc of angular diameter \(\theta\), two points separated by \(d\) have

$$|\gamma_{12}| = \left|\frac{2J_1(v)}{v}\right|, \qquad v = \frac{\pi\theta d}{\lambda},$$

which first vanishes at \(d = 1.22\,\lambda/\theta\). The Sun, 0.53° across, gives a coherence width of only about 70 µm — Young's pinhole was needed to make the light spatially coherent over his slit separation. Turn the argument round and the separation at which the fringes vanish measures the size of the source. In 1920 Michelson and Pease mounted two movable mirrors on a 6 m beam across the 100-inch telescope at Mount Wilson and found the fringes from Betelgeuse vanished at a mirror separation of about 3 m, giving an angular diameter of 0.047 arcseconds — the first measurement of the size of a star other than the Sun [11]. Modern optical and radio interferometer arrays work on the same principle.

A laser is spatially coherent across its whole beam because its light comes from one transverse mode of a cavity, not from many independent emitters. That, rather than its narrow spectrum, is why a laser beam can be focused to a diffraction-limited spot and collimated over long distances.

06A family of interferometers

Every interferometer splits light, sends the parts along different paths and recombines them. They divide either the wavefront (Young's slits, the stellar interferometer: different parts of the beam) or the amplitude (a beam splitter: the same part of the beam, sent two ways) [12]. Amplitude division keeps the full spatial coherence of the beam and is used in almost every laboratory instrument (Figure 5). In 1887 Michelson and Morley used the first of these to look for the motion of the Earth through the supposed ether and found none [13].

07Measuring length with light

In a Michelson, moving one mirror by \(\Delta z\) changes the path by \(2\Delta z\) and the phase by \(\varphi = 4\pi\Delta z/\lambda\): one full fringe per \(\lambda/2\), 316 nm for a HeNe laser. Counting fringes measures length directly in wavelengths, which is why the metre is realised through the wavelengths of stabilised lasers.

A single detector cannot tell whether the mirror moved forwards or backwards: \(\cos\varphi\) looks the same either way. Displacement-measuring interferometers therefore produce two signals 90° apart, \(\cos\varphi\) and \(\sin\varphi\), using polarisation optics; plotted against each other they trace a circle, and the direction of travel round it gives the sign (Figure 6). Interpolating the phase within a fringe gives resolutions of a nanometre or better. A second approach, heterodyne detection, uses a laser emitting two slightly different frequencies; the mirror's motion Doppler-shifts the beat between them, and counting the beat gives position — the basis of the commercial laser interferometers introduced in 1970 [14]. In air, the wavelength must be corrected for the refractive index, about 1.00027 at normal temperature and pressure, which depends on temperature, pressure and humidity [15]; ignoring it is an error of 0.27 µm per metre.

08Testing optical surfaces

Place a lens on a flat glass plate and look in reflected light: concentric Newton's rings appear, dark where the air gap is a whole number of half-wavelengths. For a surface of radius \(R\) the \(m\)-th dark ring has radius \(r_m = \sqrt{m\lambda R}\). The same idea, with a reference surface of known quality, is the everyday test of optical surfaces in the Fizeau and Twyman–Green interferometers [12, 16]. The fringes are contours of equal gap, so each fringe is a height step of \(\lambda/2\); a flat surface gives straight, evenly spaced fringes (their number set only by the tilt of the reference), a curved one gives circles, astigmatism gives saddles, and coma and spherical aberration bend the fringes (Figure 7). Modern instruments shift the reference by fractions of a wavelength and compute the height at every pixel (phase-shifting interferometry), reaching a small fraction of a nanometre.

09White-light interferometry

A laser interferometer measures height only modulo \(\lambda/2\): a step of 400 nm and one of 84 nm look the same at 633 nm. Short coherence turns into an advantage here. In coherence-scanning interferometry an interference microscope objective is scanned vertically while a camera records each pixel. With a broadband source, fringes appear only when the path to that pixel's surface point matches the reference path to within the coherence length, a micrometre or so, and the peak of the fringe envelope marks the height of the surface at that pixel without ambiguity [17]. The method measures roughness, steps, MEMS devices and thin films over millimetres with nanometre resolution (Figure 8).

10Optical coherence tomography

Apply the same idea to light scattered from inside a tissue and you get optical coherence tomography (OCT), introduced in 1991 [18]: a Michelson with the sample in one arm, where only light returning from a depth that matches the reference path interferes. The depth resolution is set by the coherence length of the source; for a Gaussian spectrum of centre \(\lambda_0\) and width \(\Delta\lambda\) [19]

$$\Delta z = \frac{2\ln 2}{\pi}\,\frac{\lambda_0^2}{\Delta\lambda}$$

in air, divided by the refractive index in tissue. Modern systems do not scan the reference mirror: they record the interference spectrum with a spectrometer, and a Fourier transform turns the spectral fringes into a depth profile, an A-scan, for every lateral position at once [20]. Each reflecting layer adds spectral fringes whose period is inversely proportional to its depth (Figure 9). Scanning the beam sideways builds a cross-section of the retina in a fraction of a second, which has made OCT routine in every eye clinic; 1310 nm sources reach deeper into scattering tissues such as skin and artery walls.

11How small a phase can be measured?

Light arrives as photons, and counting \(N\) of them has a statistical uncertainty of \(\sqrt N\) (the shot noise of Part 4). For an interferometer this sets the smallest detectable phase change,

$$\delta\varphi \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt N},$$

the shot-noise limit (also called the standard quantum limit for phase). With 1 mW at 1064 nm, \(N \approx 5\times10^{15}\) photons per second and \(\delta\varphi \approx 1.4\times10^{-8}\) rad in one second — a mirror displacement of about \(10^{-15}\) m. A gravitational-wave detector pushes every factor: kilometre arms, light bouncing hundreds of times in Fabry–Pérot cavities in each arm, and kilowatts of circulating power. On 14 September 2015 the two LIGO detectors recorded the signal of two black holes merging, a strain of \(10^{-21}\) — a change of the 4 km arm length by about \(4\times10^{-18}\) m, a few thousandths of a proton diameter [21]. Figure 10 shows how the idealised shot-noise floor depends on power, arm length and the number of round trips; real detectors are also limited by seismic and thermal noise and by radiation pressure, and now inject squeezed light to beat the shot-noise limit [22].

12Interfering intensities: photon bunching

Everything so far was first-order coherence: correlations of the field. In 1956 Hanbury Brown and Twiss showed that the intensities measured by two separate detectors are also correlated, and used it to measure stars without having to keep two optical paths stable to a fraction of a wavelength [23]. The second-order correlation

$$g^{(2)}(\tau) = \frac{\langle I(t)\,I(t+\tau)\rangle}{\langle I\rangle^2}$$

is 2 at \(\tau = 0\) for thermal light, which fluctuates on the scale of its coherence time: photons arrive bunched. Laser light, whose intensity is steady, gives \(g^{(2)} = 1\): photon arrivals are independent (Poissonian). Glauber's quantum theory of coherence explained both and predicted a third case [24]: a single atom, molecule or quantum dot cannot emit two photons at once, so \(g^{(2)}(0) = 0\) — antibunching, first observed in 1977 [25] and now the standard test of a single-photon source (Figure 11).

13Speckle: coherence as a nuisance

Shine a laser on a wall and the spot looks grainy. Every point of the image receives light from many points of the rough surface, with random path differences, and their coherent sum is a random interference pattern: speckle [26]. Fully developed speckle has a contrast \(\sigma_I/\langle I\rangle = 1\) and a grain size of about \(\lambda z/D\) for an aperture \(D\) at distance \(z\). It limits laser projection, holography and coherent imaging, and it is the main source of the grainy texture of OCT images. Averaging \(M\) independent speckle patterns — from a moving diffuser, several angles, wavelengths or polarisations — reduces the contrast to \(1/\sqrt M\) (Figure 12). Speckle is also useful: its motion measures strain, vibration and blood flow.

14Getting fringes in practice

The visibility equation of Section 01 is also a troubleshooting list. Alignment itself — overlapping the beams in position and angle — is covered in Part 5.

FactorWhat to do
Path differencekeep it well inside the coherence length; with broadband light, find zero path difference with a laser first, then scan slowly
Polarisationmake the two polarisations parallel (a polariser at the output, or a wave plate in one arm); watch for mirrors and beam splitters that change polarisation
Intensity balancea 10 : 1 ratio still gives V = 0.57; use an attenuator in the strong arm if you need full visibility
Spatial coherencewith lamps and LEDs, use a pinhole or a small source; image the source to keep both arms looking at the same point
Wavefront matchequal curvature in both arms, or the fringes become circles; tilt gives straight fringes
Stabilitya path change of λ/2 is one full fringe: isolate from vibration, enclose against air currents, keep arms short and symmetric
Stray reflectionsback surfaces of beam splitters and windows add extra fringe systems; use wedged or anti-reflection-coated optics
