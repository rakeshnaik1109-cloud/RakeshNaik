How cameras and light detectors work: from a photon to a number

Every spectrum and every micrograph ends as numbers read out of a detector. This tutorial follows the signal from the photon to the digital value: how silicon turns light into electrons and why it stops at 1.1 µm, photodiodes, photomultipliers and single-photon detectors, how CCD and CMOS sensors read their pixels and why phone videos bend propellers, where every kind of noise comes from and how CCD, EMCCD and sCMOS cameras compare, full well, dynamic range and the photon transfer curve, cooling, pixels and aliasing, colour filters, lenses and f-numbers, infrared detectors — and how dark frames and flat fields turn pictures into measurements.

This is the third instrument part of the reading path. Part 2 spread light into a spectrum and Part 3 formed an image; both ended on a detector. Here is what happens there, and why the same physics — the photon nature of light from Part 1 and the band gap of a semiconductor — sets the noise floor of every measurement in the parts that follow. 01From a photon to a number

A digital camera pixel is a small light meter, and every one does the same five things (Figure 1). Photons arrive; a fraction of them, the quantum efficiency (QE), free an electron each in the semiconductor — the photoelectric effect, explained by Einstein in 1905 [1]. The electrons are collected and stored in a potential well, which can hold only so many: the full well. At the end of the exposure the charge is converted to a voltage by an amplifier, which adds read noise. Finally an analogue-to-digital converter turns the voltage into a digital number (DN, or ADU — analogue-to-digital unit), with a conversion gain \(K\) in electrons per ADU and a small offset added so that noise never pushes the value below zero:

$$\text{DN} = \frac{\eta\,N_\text{ph}}{K} + \text{offset} ,$$

where \(\eta\) is the QE and \(N_\text{ph}\) the number of photons. Everything in this article is about one of these steps: how efficiently photons become electrons, how much noise each step adds, and what limits the largest and smallest signals.

02How silicon detects light

In a semiconductor, electrons fill a valence band separated from an empty conduction band by an energy gap \(E_g\). A photon with energy \(h\nu > E_g\) can lift an electron across, leaving a hole; an electric field inside the device (a p–n junction or a gate electrode) separates the pair before it recombines, and the electron is collected [2]. For silicon, \(E_g = 1.12\) eV at room temperature, so photons with wavelengths beyond \(\lambda = hc/E_g \approx 1.1\) µm are not detected at all.

How deep the light goes before it is absorbed matters just as much. Silicon absorbs blue light within a fraction of a micrometre but lets near-infrared light travel tens or hundreds of micrometres [3]. In a front-illuminated sensor the light enters through the electrodes and wiring on top of the pixels, which absorb and reflect much of the blue. A back-illuminated sensor is thinned and turned over, so light enters through bare, anti-reflection-coated silicon: QE reaches above 90 % across the visible [4]. At long wavelengths, a thin sensor lets light through before it is absorbed; deep-depletion sensors, tens of micrometres thick, recover near-infrared QE — important for Raman with 785 nm lasers — at the cost of higher dark current. In thin back-illuminated sensors, near-infrared light reflected between the two faces interferes and produces etaloning fringes in spectra.

03Single-point detectors: photodiodes, PMTs, APDs and SPADs

A photodiode turns light directly into a current. Its responsivity — current per watt of light — is \(R = \eta\,e\lambda/hc = \eta\,\lambda/(1239.8\ \text{nm})\) A/W, about 0.6 A/W for silicon near 900 nm [2]. It is linear over many decades, robust and cheap, and is the detector of UV–Vis instruments and power meters.

For weak light, the signal must be amplified before electronic noise swamps it. In a photomultiplier tube (PMT), a photon frees an electron from a photocathode (QE 20–40 %); the electron is accelerated onto a series of dynodes, each releasing several secondary electrons, so that one photoelectron becomes a pulse of \(10^5\)–\(10^7\) electrons [8]. PMTs count single photons, respond in nanoseconds and have large sensitive areas; they are used in fluorometers, confocal microscopes and scanning monochromators. Their gain depends steeply on the high voltage, which must therefore be very stable.

An avalanche photodiode (APD) brings gain into the semiconductor: a high reverse bias accelerates the photoelectron until it knocks free more carriers, giving gains of tens to hundreds, with some excess noise. Biased above breakdown, the same device becomes a single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD): one photon triggers a full avalanche that is detected as a digital pulse and then quenched. SPADs count photons with timing precision of tens of picoseconds and are now made in arrays (section 15) [23].

04Image sensors: CCD and CMOS

An image sensor is a grid of millions of light-sensitive pixels. The two families differ in how they read them. In the charge-coupled device (CCD), invented by Boyle and Smith in 1969 [6], the charge packets themselves are moved: clock voltages on the gate electrodes shift every row down by one, the bottom row into a serial register, and the serial register out, pixel by pixel, through a single low-noise amplifier. Charge is transferred with an efficiency above 99.9999 % per step, so the image arrives intact after thousands of transfers [4]. All pixels share one amplifier, which gives very uniform response, but reading millions of pixels through one output takes time.

In the CMOS active-pixel sensor [7], each pixel has its own amplifier, and each column its own analogue-to-digital converter, so a whole row is converted at once. CMOS sensors are fast, cheap and low in power, and can include processing on the chip. Early CMOS sensors were noisier and less uniform than CCDs; scientific CMOS (sCMOS) sensors, introduced around 2010, combined read noise near 1–2 electrons with 100 frames per second at several megapixels [22], and now dominate most scientific imaging. CCDs remain the choice where very long, cooled exposures and the lowest dark current matter, as in Raman spectroscopy and astronomy.

05Global and rolling shutters

Because a CMOS sensor reads its rows one after another, the simplest design also starts and stops each row's exposure one after another: a rolling shutter. Every row sees the scene at a slightly different moment, a few to tens of microseconds after the row above. For still scenes that does not matter; fast motion is distorted — a spinning propeller bends, a moving car leans, and a flash may light only part of the frame. A global shutter exposes all pixels at the same time, at the cost of extra transistors in each pixel and usually a little more noise. Scientific cameras offer both; for fast processes or pulsed illumination, synchronise the light with the "all rows exposing" part of a rolling shutter, or use global mode.

06Where the noise comes from

Even a perfect detector shows noise, because light arrives in photons at random times. The number counted in an exposure follows Poisson statistics: with a mean of \(S\) photo-electrons, the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{S}\) — shot noise, first analysed by Schottky for electrons in vacuum tubes [11]. The detector adds its own noise:

  • Dark current: electrons generated thermally, \(D\) per pixel per second, accumulate with the signal and carry their own shot noise \(\sqrt{Dt}\) (section 10).
  • Read noise \(\sigma_r\): added once per readout by the amplifier and converter, independent of the signal.
  • Fixed-pattern noise: pixel-to-pixel differences in offset and in sensitivity (PRNU, photo-response non-uniformity), the same in every frame and therefore removable by calibration (section 16).

Independent noise sources add in quadrature, so the signal-to-noise ratio of one pixel is [4, 5]

$$\text{SNR} = \frac{\eta N_\text{ph}}{\sqrt{F^2(\eta N_\text{ph} + Dt) + \sigma_r^2}} ,$$

where \(F\) is an excess noise factor (1 for ordinary sensors, about \(\sqrt2\) for an EMCCD). At high signal the shot noise wins and SNR \(\approx \sqrt{\eta N_\text{ph}}\): the only improvement is more light or higher QE. At low signal the read noise wins, and a camera with lower read noise is genuinely better.

07CCD, EMCCD and sCMOS compared

The electron-multiplying CCD (EMCCD) adds a gain register between the serial register and the amplifier: charge is clocked through hundreds of stages at a high voltage, and in each there is a small chance of impact ionisation, multiplying the packet by up to a thousand before it is read [9]. The read noise, divided by the gain, effectively disappears, and single photons can be seen. The multiplication is random, which adds an excess noise factor of about \(\sqrt2\) — as if the QE were halved — so EMCCDs are best at the very lowest light levels [10]. Cooled back-illuminated CCDs win for long exposures of faint, static signals; sCMOS cameras win for speed, large fields and moderate light; and consumer colour sensors, with their filters and higher noise, are far behind for quantitative work.

08An image is made of photons

Shot noise is not a defect of the camera but a property of light. A faint fluorescence image of a cell is literally a scatter of a few photons per pixel, and the structure emerges from the noise only as photons accumulate (Figure 7). This is why fluorescence microscopy is a constant negotiation between brightness, exposure time, photobleaching and the damage the light does to living cells (Part 3), and why every gain in QE and every electron of read noise saved matters.

09Full well, dynamic range and the photon transfer curve

The largest signal a pixel can hold is its full well capacity, from a few thousand electrons in small consumer pixels to several hundred thousand in large scientific ones. The smallest signal it can distinguish from nothing is about its read noise. Their ratio is the dynamic range, often quoted in decibels as \(20\log_{10}(\text{FW}/\sigma_r)\); the converter needs at least \(\log_2(\text{FW}/\sigma_r)\) bits to digitise it without throwing away the faintest steps — which is why a camera with 30 000 electrons of well and 1.5 electrons of noise needs more than 14 bits.

All of these numbers can be measured with nothing but uniform light and the camera itself, by the photon transfer method [5]: record pairs of frames at increasing light levels and plot the noise against the mean signal on logarithmic axes. Three regimes appear — a flat floor of read noise, a slope of one half where shot noise dominates, and a slope of one where pixel-to-pixel sensitivity differences dominate — ending abruptly at the full well. The slope-½ region also gives the conversion gain, since there the variance in electrons must equal the mean. The European machine-vision standard EMVA 1288 [19] specifies this measurement so that cameras from different makers can be compared.

10Dark current and cooling

Heat alone generates electron–hole pairs in a semiconductor. The rate depends exponentially on temperature, through the band gap: in silicon, generation in the depletion region goes roughly as \(T^{3/2}\exp(-E_g/2kT)\) [2, 4], so the dark current falls by about half for every 6–9 °C of cooling near room temperature. An uncooled sensor may produce hundreds of dark electrons per pixel per second; at −60 °C to −90 °C, cooled by thermoelectric (Peltier) elements or liquid nitrogen, the rate drops below a thousandth of an electron per second, and exposures of many minutes become possible — essential for Raman spectroscopy, where signals are weak and exposures long. Cooling also suppresses hot pixels, individual pixels with unusually high dark current, and the sensor must be kept in vacuum or dry gas so that it does not frost.

11Pixels, sampling and aliasing

A sensor samples the image at the pixel pitch \(a\). Sampling theory says that a pattern can be recorded faithfully only if there are at least two samples per period — the Nyquist limit [12]. Finer patterns are not merely lost: they masquerade as coarser ones, producing aliasing or moiré. Each pixel also averages the light over its area, which lowers the contrast of fine detail (the pixel's modulation transfer function, \(|\sin(\pi a/p)/(\pi a/p)|\) for a pattern of period \(p\)). In a microscope, the optics blur the image enough that a camera sampling at the Nyquist rate cannot alias (Part 3, section 16); in photography with sharp lenses, aliasing is real, and many cameras put a weak optical low-pass filter in front of the sensor to prevent it.

12Colour: Bayer filters and demosaicing

A pixel measures how much light arrived, not its colour. Most colour cameras cover the sensor with a mosaic of red, green and blue filters — the Bayer pattern, patented by Bryce Bayer at Kodak in 1976 [13] — with twice as many green as red or blue pixels, matching the eye's greater sensitivity to green. Each pixel records one colour, and the other two are interpolated from its neighbours: demosaicing [14]. Simple interpolation produces false colours at sharp edges and on fine patterns; cameras use more elaborate methods, but the information is not there. Each colour filter also blocks about two thirds of the light. For quantitative imaging — fluorescence, spectroscopy, low light — monochrome sensors are therefore preferred, and colour images are built from separate exposures through filters. Three-chip cameras, with a prism splitting the light onto three sensors, avoid both losses at higher cost.

13Lenses: f-number, depth of field and diffraction

The lens decides how much light reaches the sensor and how sharp it is. Its f-number \(N = f/D\), focal length over aperture diameter, sets the irradiance on the sensor, which scales as \(1/N^2\): each "stop" (a factor \(\sqrt2\) in \(N\)) halves the light [15]. Exposure is a three-way trade between f-number, exposure time and gain (ISO). Closing the aperture increases the depth of field — the range of distances that appear acceptably sharp, here taken as blurring no more than two pixels — but also the diffraction blur: the image of a point is an Airy disc of diameter \(2.44\lambda N\), the camera counterpart of the microscope's resolution limit (\(N \approx 1/2\text{NA}\)). Beyond a certain f-number, which is smaller for smaller pixels, every image is limited by diffraction rather than by the lens design.

15Counting and timing single photons

Some measurements need not only how many photons arrive, but when. In time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC), a pulsed laser excites the sample and a fast single-photon detector — a PMT, a hybrid detector or a SPAD — records the delay of each detected photon with picosecond precision [18]. A histogram of millions of delays gives the fluorescence decay; done at every pixel of a scanning microscope it gives fluorescence-lifetime images (FLIM), which reveal the environment of a dye and energy transfer between molecules. SPAD arrays now bring single-photon counting and timing to every pixel of a camera [23], and streak cameras, which sweep the photoelectrons across a screen, resolve events on picosecond to femtosecond scales.

16From pictures to measurements

A camera's numbers become measurements only after calibration. Work with raw, linear data: consumer image formats apply a non-linear tone curve (gamma), colour processing and compression that make intensities incomparable. Then remove what the camera adds. A dark frame, taken with no light at the same exposure and temperature, contains the offset, the dark current and the hot pixels; subtracting it removes them. A flat field, an image of perfectly uniform light, records everything that makes the response uneven — vignetting by the optics, shadows of dust on the window, and the pixel-to-pixel sensitivity differences of section 09. The corrected image is [5, 20]

$$I_\text{corr} = \frac{I_\text{raw} - I_\text{dark}}{I_\text{flat} - I_\text{dark}}\ \big\langle I_\text{flat} - I_\text{dark}\big\rangle .$$

Averaging several dark and flat frames keeps their own noise from being added to every image. The same logic is applied to spectra (Part 2, section 18), where the instrument response takes the role of the flat field.
