How microscopes work: resolution, contrast and the many ways to see a cell

A microscope has three jobs: to magnify, to resolve and to make things visible. This tutorial builds each from optics — infinity-corrected objectives and the tube lens, numerical aperture and immersion, the diffraction limit and Abbe's theory of image formation, Köhler illumination — and then follows the ideas that turned an almost invisible cell into a rich image: darkfield, phase contrast, DIC and polarised light, fluorescence, confocal, two-photon, light-sheet and TIRF microscopy, the super-resolution methods that broke the diffraction limit, and, for comparison, the electron and scanning-probe microscopes.

01What a microscope must do

Three things decide what a microscope shows. Magnification makes a small object large enough for the eye or a camera to see. Resolution decides whether two nearby details appear as two — and magnifying a blurred image only gives a larger blur ("empty magnification"). Contrast decides whether a detail can be told apart from its surroundings at all: a perfectly resolved but transparent cell is still invisible. The history of light microscopy since the 1870s is largely the story of resolution being understood (Abbe, Rayleigh) and then of contrast being invented, one method at a time, and finally of the resolution limit itself being side-stepped.

02From magnifying glass to infinity-corrected microscope

A single lens of focal length \(f\) held close to the eye magnifies by about \(250\ \text{mm}/f\), the ratio of its focal length to the closest distance at which the eye focuses comfortably. A compound microscope uses two stages. The objective forms a magnified real image of the specimen — the intermediate image — and an eyepiece (or a camera placed in that image) looks at it. Modern microscopes are infinity-corrected: the specimen sits in the front focal plane of the objective, so the light leaving the objective from each point is parallel, and a separate tube lens focuses it to the intermediate image (Figure 1). The magnification of the objective is then fixed by the ratio of the two focal lengths [16]:

$$M_\text{obj} = \frac{f_\text{tube}}{f_\text{obj}} .$$

The tube-lens focal length is chosen by each maker — 200 mm for Nikon and Leica, 180 mm for Olympus (now Evident), 165 mm for Zeiss — so objectives are interchangeable only within a system. The space of parallel light between objective and tube lens is the reason infinity optics won: filters, polarisers, the dichroic mirrors of fluorescence microscopes and beam splitters can be inserted there without shifting the focus or adding aberrations.

03Numerical aperture and immersion

The most important number engraved on an objective is not its magnification but its numerical aperture,

$$\text{NA} = n\sin\alpha ,$$

where \(\alpha\) is the half-angle of the widest cone of light the objective accepts and \(n\) the refractive index of the medium between the specimen and the front lens [16, 17]. The NA sets the resolution (section 04), the brightness of fluorescence images (the light collected from a point grows roughly as NA²) and, inversely, the depth of field. A dry objective cannot exceed NA = 1, and in practice reaches about 0.95. There is a second limit, shown in Figure 2: rays leaving the specimen at steep angles are totally reflected at the top of the coverslip if air lies above it (Part 1, section 09). Filling the gap with immersion oil of the same index as glass (1.518) removes that interface, and oil objectives reach NA 1.4–1.49. Water (1.33) and glycerol (1.47) immersion match living samples better and avoid the aberrations of focusing deep into water through oil.

04How small? The diffraction limit

Even a perfect lens cannot focus light to a point. Because it collects only part of the wave leaving a point, the image of that point is a small diffraction pattern — for a circular aperture, the Airy pattern, a bright disc surrounded by faint rings. Its intensity is \(I(v) = [2J_1(v)/v]^2\), with \(v = 2\pi\,\text{NA}\,r/\lambda\) and \(J_1\) a Bessel function [17]. This image of a point is the point-spread function (PSF). Two points can be told apart when their PSFs do not overlap too much. Rayleigh's criterion puts the peak of one on the first dark ring of the other [2]:

$$d_\text{Rayleigh} = \frac{0.61\,\lambda}{\text{NA}} ,\qquad d_\text{Abbe} = \frac{\lambda}{2\,\text{NA}} .$$

Abbe's form comes from his theory of image formation (section 05) [1]; the two differ only by the criterion used. For green light and an oil objective of NA 1.4, both give a little over 200 nm. Along the optical axis the PSF is longer: the axial resolution of a widefield microscope is roughly \(2n\lambda/\text{NA}^2\), about 600 nm for the same objective. Better resolution therefore needs a shorter wavelength or a larger NA — or, as section 15 shows, a way around the premise.

05Abbe's theory: the image is built from diffraction orders

Ernst Abbe, working with Carl Zeiss in Jena, explained in 1873 why some objectives resolved details that others, equally magnifying, did not [1]. Think of the specimen as a grating of period \(p\) lit by a plane wave. It diffracts the light into orders at angles \(\sin\theta_m = m\lambda/p\) (the grating equation of Part 2). The objective collects only the orders with \(|\sin\theta_m| \le \text{NA}\), and the image is formed by the interference of the orders it collects. If only the zero order enters, the image is uniform: the structure is lost. At least two orders are needed, which gives the smallest resolvable period

$$p_\text{min} = \frac{\lambda}{\text{NA}}\ \text{(axial illumination)},\qquad p_\text{min} = \frac{\lambda}{2\,\text{NA}}\ \text{(oblique illumination, or a condenser of equal NA)} .$$

The objective acts as a filter in the spatial frequencies of the specimen: the back focal plane holds the specimen's diffraction pattern — its Fourier transform — and the image is the transform back [18]. This view underlies phase contrast (section 08), structured illumination (section 15) and much of modern image processing.

06Köhler illumination

How the specimen is lit matters as much as how it is imaged. In 1893 August Köhler introduced the arrangement still used in every research microscope [3]. A collector lens images the lamp filament onto the condenser's aperture diaphragm; the condenser then sends each filament point through the specimen as a parallel beam, so the filament is nowhere in focus in the image and the field is evenly lit. A field diaphragm near the lamp is imaged onto the specimen. The microscope therefore has two interleaved sets of conjugate planes (Figure 5): the field planes (field diaphragm, specimen, intermediate image, camera) and the aperture planes (lamp filament, aperture diaphragm, the objective's back focal plane, the eyepoint).

Each diaphragm has one job. Close the field diaphragm until it just frames the area being viewed: less stray light and glare. The aperture diaphragm sets the condenser NA: open it to resolve the finest detail (the condenser NA adds to the objective's, as Abbe's oblique case shows), close it to gain contrast and depth of field. Setting it to about 70–80 % of the objective's NA is a common compromise.

07The contrast problem: brightfield, staining and darkfield

In brightfield, the specimen is seen by the light it absorbs. Most cells absorb almost nothing in the visible: they change the phase of the light passing through them, because their refractive index (1.36–1.40) differs slightly from that of the surrounding water, but not its amplitude. Eyes and cameras record intensity only, so an unstained cell is nearly invisible. The first remedy was chemical: stains such as haematoxylin and eosin, which bind to particular structures and absorb, at the price of killing and fixing the cell. Closing the condenser aperture or defocusing slightly makes transparent objects faintly visible, but sacrifices resolution.

Darkfield takes the opposite route: the specimen is lit by a hollow cone of light steeper than the objective can accept, so no direct light enters the objective, and only light scattered by the specimen does. Small or transparent objects then shine against a black background. Darkfield is excellent for detecting tiny particles — bacteria, flagella, and the plasmonic nanoparticles of Part 6, whose scattering colours it shows directly — but it emphasises edges and dust and gives little information about the inside of a cell.

08Phase contrast and DIC

Frits Zernike's phase contrast, published in 1942, turned phase differences into intensity differences [4]. A weakly scattering specimen transmits a strong undiffracted wave and a weak diffracted wave that lags it by about a quarter of a period. In the objective's back focal plane the two are separated — the undiffracted light passes through a small region set by an annular condenser stop. A phase ring there shifts the undiffracted light by a further quarter wave (and dims it), so the two now interfere destructively or constructively, and regions of higher optical path appear darker (positive phase contrast) or brighter (negative). Phase contrast is simple and robust and is used on almost every cell-culture microscope; its characteristic artefacts are bright halos around objects.

Differential interference contrast (DIC), developed by Georges Nomarski in the 1950s [5], splits the illumination with a Wollaston (Nomarski) prism into two orthogonally polarised beams displaced sideways by less than the resolution, sends both through the specimen, recombines them with a second prism and analyses them with a polariser. The image shows the gradient of the optical path along the shear direction: a pseudo-relief, as if the cell were lit from one side. DIC gives thin optical sections, no halos and full use of the NA, but needs polarisation-preserving optics and cannot be used through birefringent plastic dishes.

09Polarised-light microscopy

Between crossed polarisers, an isotropic specimen stays dark. A birefringent one (Part 1, section 08) splits the light into two polarisations travelling at different speeds; after a thickness \(t\) one lags the other by the retardation \(\Gamma = \Delta n\, t\), and the analyser passes a fraction

$$\frac{I}{I_0} = \sin^2 2\theta\ \sin^2\!\frac{\pi\Gamma}{\lambda} ,$$

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the specimen's axis and the polariser. The specimen goes dark whenever its axis lines up with either polariser, which reveals its orientation. In white light, each wavelength is transmitted differently and the specimen shows interference colours that depend only on \(\Gamma\) — the sequence charted by Michel-Lévy [25] and used by geologists to identify minerals in 30 µm thin sections. In biology, polarised light reveals ordered structures without staining: collagen, muscle fibres, the mitotic spindle, starch grains, and crystals such as amyloid deposits stained with Congo red.

10Fluorescence microscopy and the filter cube

Fluorescence turned microscopy from seeing shapes into seeing molecules. A fluorophore absorbs light and re-emits it at a longer wavelength — the Stokes shift — after a few nanoseconds [20]. Label a protein with a fluorescent antibody or a fluorescent protein such as GFP, and only that protein glows, against a dark background. Because the emission is much weaker than the excitation — often by a factor of a million — everything depends on separating the two. The epi-fluorescence microscope does it with a filter cube in the infinity space: an excitation filter selects the band that excites the dye, a dichroic mirror at 45° reflects it down through the objective, and the same objective collects the emission, which passes back through the dichroic (it transmits the longer wavelengths) and an emission filter that blocks any remaining excitation light. Only light scattered or emitted by the specimen travels back towards the camera; the strong excitation goes forward, away from it.

Choosing filters is a balance between collecting as much of the dye's emission as possible and rejecting the excitation and the emission of other dyes in the same sample ("bleed-through"). Two practical limits recur: photobleaching, the irreversible destruction of fluorophores under illumination, which limits how long and how brightly a sample can be imaged; and out-of-focus light: a widefield microscope excites the whole depth of the sample, so thick samples give blurred, hazy images — the problem the next three sections solve.

11Confocal microscopy

Marvin Minsky's confocal microscope (patented in 1957) [6] rejects out-of-focus light with a pinhole. The specimen is illuminated by a focused spot, and the light it sends back is focused onto a pinhole in a plane conjugate to the focus. Light from the focal plane passes; light from above or below is defocused at the pinhole and mostly blocked (Figure 9). Scanning the spot across the sample point by point — with galvanometer mirrors in the laser-scanning confocal microscope, or with thousands of pinholes at once on a spinning disc — builds up an optical section, and a stack of sections at different depths gives a three-dimensional image [15].

The pinhole size is quoted in Airy units (AU), the diameter of the Airy disc projected into the pinhole plane. At about 1 AU the pinhole passes most of the in-focus light and gives an optical section of about 0.8 µm with a high-NA oil objective. Closing it further thins the section a little and sharpens the lateral resolution towards \(0.37\lambda/\text{NA}\) (full width at half maximum, against \(0.51\lambda/\text{NA}\) in widefield) [15], but throws away signal. Confocal microscopy is the workhorse for fixed and live tissue, and the basis of confocal Raman microscopy (Part 7).

12Two-photon and multiphoton microscopy

A molecule can absorb two photons at once if they arrive within about a femtosecond of each other, each carrying half the energy needed — a process predicted by Maria Göppert-Mayer in 1931 [26]. The rate grows with the square of the intensity, so with an ordinary lamp it never happens; with a femtosecond laser focused by a high-NA objective it happens only at the focus. Denk, Strickler and Webb built the first two-photon microscope in 1990 [7]. Because the excitation summed over each plane falls off as \(1/(1 + z^2/z_R^2)\) away from the focus, while one-photon excitation does not fall off at all, two-photon microscopy gives optical sections without a pinhole, and all emitted light — even light scattered on its way out — can be collected. Its near-infrared excitation (700–1100 nm) scatters less in tissue, so it images hundreds of micrometres deep in living brain; three-photon excitation near 1300 nm reaches deeper still [27].

13Light-sheet microscopy

A confocal or two-photon microscope illuminates the whole depth of the sample to image one plane. A light-sheet microscope illuminates only the plane being imaged, with a thin sheet of light from the side, and records it at once with a camera through a second objective at right angles. Huisken and colleagues introduced the method as selective plane illumination microscopy (SPIM) in 2004 [8]. Photobleaching and photodamage fall dramatically, and whole embryos can be followed for days. The sheet is a focused beam, so its thickness and usable length are tied together: a Gaussian sheet of waist \(w_0\) stays thinner than \(\sqrt2\,w_0\) only over \(2z_R = 2\pi w_0^2 n/\lambda\). Thin sheets for fine optical sectioning are therefore short; Bessel beams and lattice light sheets [28] stretch the thin region further.

14TIRF: imaging the bottom 200 nm

Total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy, developed by Daniel Axelrod [9], illuminates only a thin layer next to the coverslip. Light striking the glass–water interface beyond the critical angle is totally reflected, but an evanescent field — the same field that drives ATR in Part 2 and SPR in Part 10 — extends a short distance into the sample, with an intensity decaying as \(e^{-z/d}\) and

$$d = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta - n_2^2}} ,$$

typically 70–200 nm. Only fluorophores in that layer are excited: membrane proteins, adhesion sites, vesicles fusing with the membrane and single molecules on a surface stand out against a nearly black background. In objective-type TIRF the laser is brought in at the edge of a high-NA oil objective, which must have NA above the index of the cell (about 1.37), in practice 1.45–1.49.

15Beyond the diffraction limit

The diffraction limit assumes that all molecules within a PSF emit at the same time and are excited in the same way. Super-resolution methods break one of those assumptions, and their inventors shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Betzig, Hell and Moerner):

  • STED (stimulated emission depletion) [10]: a doughnut-shaped beam forces excited molecules back to the ground state by stimulated emission everywhere except at its dark centre. The fluorescing spot shrinks as the depletion intensity rises, \(d \approx \lambda/(2\,\text{NA}\sqrt{1 + I/I_\text{sat}})\), to a few tens of nanometres.
  • SIM (structured illumination) [11]: illuminating with a fine stripe pattern shifts otherwise inaccessible spatial frequencies into the objective's pupil as moiré fringes (Abbe's picture again, section 05). Images at several pattern positions and orientations are combined to give twice the resolution, gently enough for live cells.
  • Single-molecule localisation — PALM [12] and STORM [13]: fluorophores are switched on a few at a time, so their images do not overlap, and the centre of each is located far more precisely than the width of the PSF. The precision improves as \(1/\sqrt{N}\) with the photons collected [14]; thousands of frames are combined into one image with a resolution of 10–30 nm. MINFLUX pushes this to a few nanometres by locating molecules with a doughnut beam [29].

16Matching the camera to the microscope

A camera samples the image on a grid of pixels. To record everything the optics resolve, the pixel size projected back into the sample — the camera pixel divided by the total magnification — must be no larger than about half the resolution (the Nyquist criterion; 2.3 pixels per resolution element is a common rule) [15, 16]. A 6.5 µm sCMOS pixel behind a 60× objective samples the sample every 108 nm, well matched to a resolution of about 230 nm; the same camera at 20× (325 nm pixels) undersamples an NA 0.75 objective's 450 nm resolution. Larger pixels collect more light per pixel, so over-sampling costs signal. How cameras turn photons into numbers, and where their noise comes from, is the subject of Part 4.

17Electron microscopes

If resolution is limited by wavelength, use a shorter one. Electrons accelerated through a voltage \(V\) have a de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = h/\sqrt{2m_eeV(1 + eV/2m_ec^2)}\): 2.5 pm at 200 kV, some 200 000 times shorter than green light. Knoll and Ruska built the first electron microscope in 1931–1932 [23]. Magnetic lenses suffer from aberrations that glass lenses correct easily, so for decades the resolution was limited by the lenses, not the wavelength; aberration correctors now bring the best transmission instruments below 0.1 nm [21].

  • Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sends a broad beam through a specimen thinner than about 100 nm and images the transmitted electrons: internal structure, crystal lattices, and, in cryo-EM, frozen-hydrated proteins reconstructed in three dimensions at near-atomic resolution.
  • Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) scans a fine beam (0.5–30 kV) over a surface and collects secondary or backscattered electrons: surface topography with nanometre resolution and great depth of field. It is the standard way to check nanostructures such as the SERS substrates and gratings of later parts. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) in an SEM adds elemental maps.

The price is a vacuum, samples that must be fixed, dried or frozen, and, for insulators, a conductive coating — no live cells.

18Scanning-probe microscopes

Scanning-probe microscopes do without lenses altogether. A sharp tip is scanned across the surface and an interaction between tip and sample is recorded point by point. The scanning tunnelling microscope (STM, 1982) [24] measures the tunnelling current between a metal tip and a conducting surface, which changes by about a factor of ten for every 0.1 nm of distance, and resolves single atoms. The atomic force microscope (AFM, 1986) [22] measures the force on a tip at the end of a flexible cantilever, usually through the deflection of a laser beam reflected from its back. It works on insulators and in liquid, gives true heights with sub-nanometre vertical resolution, and is used to measure the roughness and thickness of thin films, image DNA and proteins, and probe mechanical properties. Its lateral resolution is set by the tip radius, typically a few nanometres. Optical methods built on probes — near-field scanning optical microscopy and tip-enhanced Raman (TERS, Part 7) — combine the tip's resolution with optical contrast.
