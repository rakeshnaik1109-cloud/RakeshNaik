How spectrometers work: prisms, gratings, interferometers and the instruments built from them

A spectrum is only as good as the instrument that records it. This tutorial opens the box: how a prism and a grating separate colours, why gratings are blazed and orders overlap, how slits, mirrors and pixels set the resolution and how much light gets through, and how echelles, Fourier-transform interferometers and Fabry–Pérot etalons push further. It then walks through the instruments found in a lab — UV–Vis, fluorescence, Raman and FTIR — and ends with calibration, so every number on a spectrum can be traced back to the hardware.

Part 1 built light from Maxwell's equations: refraction, interference, diffraction and polarisation. This part puts those ideas to work in the instruments that turn light into a spectrum. The next part, Vibrational spectroscopy, explains what the peaks of an infrared or Raman spectrum mean; here the question is how the spectrum was recorded in the first place, and what the instrument has done to it on the way. Every figure is calculated from the equations in the text, so the numbers can be checked. Numbers in square brackets refer to the references.

01What a spectrometer does

A spectrometer measures how much light there is at each wavelength. Whatever its size or price, it does four jobs (Figure 1). It collects light from a source or a sample, usually through an entrance slit or an optical fibre that defines where the light comes from. It separates the wavelengths, by refraction in a prism, by diffraction from a grating, or by interference in an interferometer. It detects the separated light, with a single detector behind an exit slit or with an array that records many wavelengths at once. And it is calibrated, so that each detector reading is tied to a known wavelength and, when needed, to an absolute intensity.

Three numbers describe how well it does them. The spectral range is the band of wavelengths it covers. The resolution \(\Delta\lambda\) is the smallest wavelength difference it can separate, often quoted as the resolving power \(R = \lambda/\Delta\lambda\). The throughput is how much of the light that reaches it ends up on the detector. The rest of this article is largely about how these three trade against each other.

02Splitting light by refraction: the prism

Newton separated sunlight into colours with a prism in 1666 and described it in 1672 [1]. The prism works because the refractive index of glass depends on wavelength — dispersion, introduced in Part 1. Blue light, with the higher index, is bent more at each face than red. For a prism of apex angle \(A\), the deviation is smallest when the ray crosses the prism symmetrically, and then [2]

$$n(\lambda) = \frac{\sin\!\big[(A + D_\text{min})/2\big]}{\sin(A/2)} ,$$

where \(D_\text{min}\) is the minimum deviation. Measuring \(D_\text{min}\) at several wavelengths is still a standard way to measure \(n(\lambda)\). The index of optical glasses is described by the Sellmeier formula, \(n^2 - 1 = \sum_j B_j\lambda^2/(\lambda^2 - C_j)\), with coefficients published by the glass makers [4] and, for crystalline materials, by careful measurements such as Malitson's for fused silica and calcium fluoride [5, 6].

How finely can a prism separate two wavelengths? If the beam fills the prism, the answer depends only on the length of glass at its base, \(b\), and on how fast the index changes [2, 3]:

$$R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = b\left|\frac{dn}{d\lambda}\right| .$$

A 50 mm base of the dense flint SF11 gives \(R \approx 7600\) near 589 nm, enough to separate the two yellow sodium lines 0.6 nm apart with room to spare; a crown glass such as N-BK7, which disperses less, gives about 2100. The Abbe number \(V_d = (n_d - 1)/(n_F - n_C)\), calculated from the indices at three standard lines, sums this up: the lower \(V_d\), the stronger the dispersion.

Prisms have two drawbacks for spectroscopy: their dispersion is strongly non-linear (they spread the blue far more than the red), and their resolving power is limited by how much glass can be used. They survive where their strengths matter — no overlapping orders, high transmission, and use as order separators (cross-dispersers) in echelle spectrometers (section 10). For most work, the grating has replaced them.

03Splitting light by diffraction: the grating equation

A diffraction grating is a surface with many parallel grooves, spaced \(d\) apart — from about 30 to 3600 per millimetre. Light scattered by neighbouring grooves arrives in step only in particular directions: where the path difference between them is a whole number \(m\) of wavelengths. For light arriving at angle \(\alpha\) and leaving at angle \(\beta\), both measured from the grating normal, this gives the grating equation [7, 8]

$$m\lambda = d\,(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta) .$$

Here the angles carry signs: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are positive on the same side of the normal, so the specular reflection, \(m = 0\), leaves at \(\beta = -\alpha\) for every wavelength. Each non-zero order \(m\) spreads the wavelengths out; an order exists only if \(|\sin\beta| \le 1\), so a fine grating may have only one order or none at a long wavelength.

Differentiating the grating equation at fixed \(\alpha\) gives the angular dispersion,

$$\frac{d\beta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d\cos\beta} ,$$

which grows with the order and the groove density. And because every one of the \(N\) illuminated grooves contributes, the diffraction peak of each wavelength is very narrow. Two wavelengths are just resolved when the peak of one falls on the first zero of the other, which gives the resolving power [3, 7]

$$R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN .$$

A 25 mm wide grating with 600 grooves/mm has 15 000 grooves: in first order it can in principle separate wavelengths 0.04 nm apart at 600 nm — twice as finely as the SF11 prism above and seven times better than one of N-BK7. Whether an instrument reaches that depends on the rest of the optics (section 08).

Gratings are made in two ways. Ruled gratings are cut groove by groove with a diamond tool; their grooves can be given a sawtooth shape (section 04). Holographic gratings are recorded as an interference pattern in photoresist and etched; their grooves are smoother and more regular, which gives less scattered light and no "ghost" lines from periodic ruling errors, and they can be made on curved surfaces with corrected aberrations [7, 8]. Most commercial gratings are replicas, moulded from a master.

04Blaze: putting the light where you want it

A grating with symmetric grooves sends most of the light into the useless zero order. A blazed grating has sawtooth grooves whose facets are tilted by the blaze angle \(\theta_B\). Each facet acts as a small mirror, and when the direction of specular reflection from the facets coincides with a diffraction order, that order gets most of the light. In the Littrow arrangement, where the diffracted light returns along the incoming beam (\(\alpha = \beta = \theta_B\)), this happens at the blaze wavelength [7]

$$\lambda_B = \frac{2d\sin\theta_B}{m} .$$

The same facets therefore blaze the second order at \(\lambda_B/2\), the third at \(\lambda_B/3\), and so on. In the scalar theory, which treats the facets as ideal tilted mirrors, the efficiency of order \(m\) is \(\eta_m = \mathrm{sinc}^2(m - \lambda_B/\lambda)\) with \(\mathrm{sinc}\,x = \sin\pi x/\pi x\): 100 % at the blaze, falling to 50 % at about \(0.69\lambda_B\) and \(1.8\lambda_B\) in first order. Real gratings fall short of this, and their efficiency depends on polarisation, because the grooves treat light polarised along them and across them differently (TE and TM, Part 1, section 10). Manufacturers publish measured curves for both [7, 8].

05Overlapping orders and how to sort them

The grating equation fixes only the product \(m\lambda\). Light of 600 nm in first order, 300 nm in second order and 200 nm in third order all leave at the same angle and land on the same pixel. In a spectrometer calibrated for first order, a strong ultraviolet line therefore reappears as a false line at twice its wavelength. The range over which one order is free of the next is the free spectral range, \(\lambda/m\): from 400 to 800 nm in first order, but only from 400 to 600 nm in second.

The cure is an order-sorting filter: a long-pass filter that blocks the short wavelengths before they reach the part of the detector where they would be mistaken for long ones. Compact CCD spectrometers often have a stepped or graded filter bonded over the long-wavelength half of the detector, so that the blue half still sees the ultraviolet [7].

06From a grating to an instrument: the classic layouts

A grating diffracts parallel light into parallel light. To make an instrument, optics must turn the diverging light from the entrance slit into a parallel beam (the collimator) and focus each diffracted direction to a sharp image of the slit on the detector (the camera). The image of the slit at each wavelength is a spectral line; the spectrum is a row of slit images, one per wavelength. The main layouts differ in how they do this with the fewest surfaces and the least aberration (Figure 6).

  • Czerny–Turner [12]: two concave mirrors and a plane grating. Choosing the mirror angles so that the coma of one cancels that of the other gives sharp lines over a wide field. It is the standard laboratory spectrograph and monochromator.
  • Crossed Czerny–Turner: the same parts with the beams crossing, for a compact footprint. Most fibre-coupled mini-spectrometers use it.
  • Littrow: one lens or mirror collimates and focuses, and the grating sends the chosen order back almost along the incoming beam. It is compact and works near the blaze condition.
  • Concave grating: the grooves sit on a concave mirror, which disperses and focuses at once. Rowland showed that slit, grating and spectrum then lie on one circle of half the grating's radius [16]; holographic gratings with varied groove spacing flatten the spectrum onto a flat detector. Fewer surfaces mean less loss, which matters in the ultraviolet and vacuum ultraviolet.
  • Echelle with a cross-disperser: a coarse grating at a steep angle in high orders, with a prism spreading the orders apart (section 10).

07Monochromator or spectrograph?

The same optics can be used in two ways. A monochromator has an exit slit in the focal plane and a single detector behind it, usually a photomultiplier; rotating the grating sweeps the spectrum across the exit slit, one wavelength at a time. It selects one wavelength well — with low stray light, and with a detector that can be very sensitive — and it is used to illuminate a sample with a chosen wavelength (as in the excitation side of a fluorometer, section 14). A spectrograph has no exit slit: an array detector in the focal plane records the whole spectrum in one exposure. It is faster, since every wavelength is measured at the same time, and it has no moving parts during a measurement. The CCD spectrometers on most lab benches are spectrographs.

In both, the spread of wavelengths along the focal plane follows from the angular dispersion and the camera focal length \(f\). It is usually quoted as the reciprocal linear dispersion, in nm per mm [7, 9]:

$$\frac{d\lambda}{dx} = \frac{d\cos\beta}{m f} .$$

A 600 grooves/mm grating in first order with a 150 mm camera gives about 10 nm/mm: a 25 mm detector covers some 250 nm. With 1800 grooves/mm and 500 mm, the same detector covers about 25 nm, with ten times finer detail.

08Resolution: slit, dispersion and pixels

Three things blur a line on the detector. The first is the width \(w\) of the entrance slit: its image is (for equal collimator and camera focal lengths) the same width, and spans a wavelength range \(w \times d\lambda/dx\), the bandpass. The second is the optics: aberrations and diffraction add a blur of their own, typically a few to tens of micrometres. The third is the detector: a line cannot be sharper than about two pixels, and to measure its shape it needs several. The narrowest line the instrument records is set by the largest of the three, and the resolving power of the grating, \(mN\), is only the ceiling [7, 9].

09Throughput, étendue and the price of resolution

The amount of light an optical system can accept is set by its étendue, \(G = A\,\Omega\): the area of the entrance aperture times the solid angle of the cone it accepts. Étendue cannot be increased by any passive optics; at best it is conserved. For a slit spectrometer, \(A\) is the slit area and \(\Omega\) is set by the f-number of the collimator. Narrowing the slit to improve the resolution therefore reduces the étendue in proportion: resolution and throughput trade against each other. Jacquinot compared prisms, gratings and Fabry–Pérot etalons on this basis in 1954 and showed that, at the same resolving power, instruments without a slit can accept far more light [10]. That is the Jacquinot advantage of the Fourier-transform and Fabry–Pérot instruments below.

Two practical rules follow. Match the f-number of the light coming in (from a fibre or a lens) to that of the spectrometer: a faster cone overfills the optics and becomes stray light, a slower one wastes étendue. And choose the slit no narrower than the resolution you need: halving it halves the light for a gain you may not use.

10Echelle spectrometers: high orders in two dimensions

The resolving power of a grating can be written \(R = mN = Nd(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta)/\lambda\), which depends only on the width \(W = Nd\) of the grating and the angles. In Littrow at the blaze, \(R = 2W\sin\theta_B/\lambda\): for the highest resolution, use the grating at a steep angle. An echelle (French for ladder) is a coarse grating, typically 30–80 grooves/mm, blazed at 63.4° (an "R2" echelle, \(\tan\theta_B = 2\)) and used in orders 30 to over 100 [17]. A 50 mm echelle at 500 nm reaches \(R \approx 180\,000\).

The price is a tiny free spectral range: a few nanometres per order, so many orders overlap. A prism (or a second, low-dispersion grating) at right angles to the echelle separates them, and the spectrum falls on a two-dimensional detector as a stack of short, nearly horizontal stripes — an echellogram. Echelle spectrographs record a wide range at high resolution in one exposure, which is why they are used for elemental analysis by optical emission (ICP-OES), for laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy and in astronomy.

11Fourier-transform spectrometers

A Fourier-transform (FT) spectrometer does not separate wavelengths in space at all. It sends the light into a Michelson interferometer [13]: a beamsplitter divides it between a fixed mirror and a moving one, and the two beams recombine on a single detector. When the moving mirror has introduced an optical path difference \(\delta\), each wavenumber \(\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda\) interferes with itself and contributes a cosine to the detector signal. The recorded interferogram is therefore the cosine transform of the spectrum \(B(\tilde\nu)\) [11]:

$$I(\delta) = \tfrac12 I(0) + \int_0^\infty B(\tilde\nu)\cos(2\pi\tilde\nu\delta)\,d\tilde\nu , \qquad B(\tilde\nu) \propto \int_0^{\delta_\text{max}} \big[I(\delta) - \tfrac12 I(0)\big]\cos(2\pi\tilde\nu\delta)\,d\delta .$$

A computer inverts it with the fast Fourier transform [14]. The resolution is set by how far the mirror travels: two lines are resolved when they drift one fringe apart over the scan, \(\Delta\tilde\nu \approx 1/\delta_\text{max}\) [11]. Cutting the interferogram off abruptly gives each line sinc-shaped side lobes; multiplying it by a smooth apodisation function before the transform removes them, at the cost of slightly wider lines.

FT instruments have three classic advantages, which is why nearly all infrared spectrometers are FTIRs [11]:

  • Multiplex (Fellgett) advantage. Every wavelength reaches the detector for the whole measurement, instead of one at a time. When the noise comes from the detector rather than the light — as with infrared detectors — the signal-to-noise ratio improves by roughly the square root of the number of spectral elements.
  • Throughput (Jacquinot) advantage. A circular aperture replaces the slit; for a given resolving power it passes much more light (section 09) [10].
  • Wavenumber precision (Connes advantage). A helium–neon laser travels through the interferometer with the infrared beam, and its fringes trigger the sampling at exact intervals of path difference. Sampling at every zero crossing of the 632.8 nm fringes sets the highest measurable wavenumber at 15 803 cm⁻¹ and ties the wavenumber axis to the laser.

12Fabry–Pérot etalons and tunable filters

Two parallel, partly reflecting mirrors a distance \(l\) apart transmit only the wavelengths for which a round trip is a whole number of wavelengths: the Fabry–Pérot etalon [15]. Its transmission (the Airy function) is

$$T = \frac{1}{1 + F\sin^2(\delta/2)},\qquad \delta = \frac{4\pi n l\cos\theta}{\lambda},\qquad F = \frac{4R}{(1-R)^2} ,$$

with \(R\) the mirror reflectance. The peaks repeat every free spectral range \(\Delta\lambda_\text{FSR} = \lambda^2/2nl\), and their width is the free spectral range divided by the finesse \(\mathcal{F} = \pi\sqrt{R}/(1-R)\) [2, 3]. With good mirrors the finesse reaches hundreds, and the resolving power \(\mathcal{F}\cdot 2nl/\lambda\) can exceed a million, which is why etalons are used to measure laser linewidths and the fine structure of spectral lines. Like the FT spectrometer, an etalon has no slit and enjoys the Jacquinot advantage [10].

The same principle, and others, make tunable filters that select one band at a time without moving a grating: liquid-crystal tunable filters (stacks of birefringent plates and polarisers), acousto-optic tunable filters in which a sound wave in a crystal acts as a tunable grating [24], and linear variable filters whose passband changes along the filter. They trade resolution for speed and simplicity, and are common in imaging spectrometers.

13UV–Vis absorption spectrophotometers

The UV–Vis spectrophotometer measures how much light a sample absorbs. A deuterium lamp (ultraviolet) and a tungsten–halogen lamp (visible and near-infrared) feed a monochromator; the selected band passes through the sample and onto a detector, and the absorbance follows from the Beer–Lambert law,

$$A = -\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0} = \varepsilon c\,\ell ,$$

where \(I_0\) and \(I\) are the light reaching the detector through the reference and the sample, \(\varepsilon\) the molar absorption coefficient, \(c\) the concentration and \(\ell\) the path length. A single-beam instrument measures the reference and the sample one after the other; a double-beam instrument splits the light and measures both at once, which cancels lamp drift. Array-detector instruments ("diode-array") pass white light through the sample first and disperse it afterwards, recording the whole spectrum in a fraction of a second.

Two instrumental effects distort the absorbance, and both are easy to underestimate:

  • Spectral bandwidth. The instrument averages the transmission over its bandpass. If that is not small compared with the width of the absorption band, peaks are measured too low. A rule of thumb is a bandwidth no more than a tenth of the band's natural width.
  • Stray light. A small fraction \(s\) of light at other wavelengths reaches the detector whatever the sample does. The measured absorbance becomes \(-\log_{10}[(T + s)/(1 + s)]\) and can never exceed about \(-\log_{10}s\): with 0.1 % stray light, readings flatten near A = 3. Stray light is measured with sharp cut-off filters that block all light at the test wavelength [19].

14Fluorescence spectrometers

A spectrofluorometer has two monochromators. The excitation monochromator selects the wavelength that excites the sample; the emission monochromator, usually at 90° to the excitation beam so that little excitation light enters it, analyses the light the sample emits [18]. Scanning the emission at fixed excitation gives an emission spectrum; scanning the excitation while watching one emission wavelength gives an excitation spectrum, which for a pure compound resembles its absorption spectrum. A reference detector watches the lamp so that its spectrum and drift can be divided out, and emission spectra must be corrected for the wavelength response of the emission side before they can be compared between instruments.

Three artefacts recur. The Raman band of the solvent appears at a fixed energy shift from the excitation (about 3400 cm⁻¹ for water, so near 397 nm when exciting at 350 nm) and moves when the excitation moves, unlike true fluorescence. Second-order light from the monochromators puts copies of the excitation at twice its wavelength. And the inner-filter effect: in a concentrated sample the excitation is absorbed before it reaches the part of the cuvette the emission optics see, and the emission is re-absorbed on its way out. For the usual right-angle geometry, viewing the centre of a 1 cm cuvette, the observed signal is reduced by about \(10^{-(A_\text{ex} + A_\text{em})/2}\) [18], so the signal stops being proportional to concentration above an absorbance of about 0.1.

15Raman spectrometers: the hardware

Raman scattering, and its many variants, were the subject of Part 7 of the reading path; here is what the instrument needs [21]. A narrow-linewidth laser excites the sample; a filter removes the elastically scattered laser light, which is a million or more times stronger than the Raman signal; and a spectrograph with a cooled CCD records what remains. Modern instruments use thin-film edge or notch filters with optical densities of 6 or more at the laser line, which let a single spectrograph replace the double and triple monochromators once needed to reject the laser.

Raman spectra are plotted against the Raman shift \(\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda_0 - 1/\lambda_s\), in cm⁻¹, but the spectrograph disperses linearly in wavelength. Since \(\Delta\tilde\nu = \Delta\lambda/\lambda^2\), the same grating and detector cover fewer cm⁻¹, with finer steps, at longer wavelengths. That decides the choice of grating for each laser. It also explains a practical limit of 785 nm and 830 nm excitation: the C–H stretching region near 3000 cm⁻¹ lands beyond 1000 nm, where silicon CCDs lose their sensitivity. Polystyrene and other materials with tabulated Raman shifts are used to check the shift axis [22].

16Infrared: FTIR and ATR

Mid-infrared spectrometers are almost all FTIRs (section 11), with a glowing ceramic source, a KBr beamsplitter and a DTGS (pyroelectric) or liquid-nitrogen-cooled MCT detector [11]. The sample is what changes. Transmission through thin films, KBr pellets or liquid cells works but needs preparation, because water and many solids absorb so strongly that only micrometres of sample can be used. Attenuated total reflection (ATR) avoids that: the sample is pressed against a crystal of high index, the infrared beam is totally reflected inside the crystal, and only the evanescent field — the field that leaks a short distance beyond a totally reflecting surface (Part 1, section 09) — probes the sample. Its intensity decays with a penetration depth [20]

$$d_p = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi n_1\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - (n_2/n_1)^2}} ,$$

where \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are the indices of the crystal and the sample and \(\theta\) the angle of incidence. A few micrometres of sample are probed whatever its thickness, so liquids, pastes, powders and films are measured as they are. Because \(d_p\) grows with wavelength, ATR spectra show stronger bands at low wavenumber than transmission spectra; instrument software corrects this before library searches.

18Calibrating wavelength and intensity

A spectrograph knows only pixel numbers. Wavelength calibration ties pixels to wavelengths with the emission lines of a lamp whose wavelengths are known precisely — mercury, argon, neon or their mixtures [23]. The line centres are found to a fraction of a pixel, and a polynomial \(\lambda(p)\) is fitted to them. A straight line is not enough: \(\cos\beta\) changes across the detector and the focal surface is never perfectly flat, so the dispersion is curved, and a quadratic or cubic polynomial is usual. The residuals of the fit, a small fraction of a pixel when all is well, are the honest measure of the wavelength accuracy. FT instruments are calibrated by their reference laser instead (section 11), and Raman shifts are checked against standards [22].

Intensity calibration corrects for the instrument's response — the grating efficiency, the transmission of the optics and the quantum efficiency of the detector, all of which change with wavelength (Figures 4 and 5). It is measured with a source of known spectral output: a calibrated tungsten–halogen or deuterium lamp, or a certified luminescent glass. Before either, every measurement needs a dark spectrum (the detector's signal with no light) subtracted, and for absorption a reference spectrum to divide by. These corrections, and what comes after them — baseline removal, smoothing and peak fitting — were covered from the analysis side in Part 6.
