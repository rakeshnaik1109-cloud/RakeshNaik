Surface plasmon resonance from first principles: why TM, and how prisms, gratings and fibres excite it

Why can a sheet of gold carry a wave of light bound to its surface? Why does only one polarisation launch it, and why can light not launch it directly? Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensors watch molecules arrive at a metal surface without labels and in real time. They do it by exciting a very particular kind of wave — a surface plasmon — that lives on the boundary between a metal and a liquid, and by noticing how that wave changes when the liquid changes. This article builds SPR from the ground up: why a metal can carry such a wave at all, why only one polarisation of light can launch it, why light cannot launch it directly, and how prisms, gratings, waveguides and optical fibres get around that. Every curve below is calculated, so the numbers can be checked against the equations in the text. The physics of light in metals and of TE and TM polarisation was set up in Part 1 (sections 11 and 10); Part 5 introduced surface plasmons briefly. The next part, Putting antibodies on an SPR sensor, takes the chip built here and turns it into a biosensor. Numbers in square brackets refer to the references.

01What SPR measures

An SPR sensor is a refractometer for a very thin slice of liquid. Light is used to launch a surface wave on a gold film; the wave only exists at one precise combination of angle and wavelength, and that combination depends on the refractive index of the medium within a few hundred nanometres of the gold. When molecules bind to the surface, the local refractive index rises (protein ≈ 1.45, water ≈ 1.333), and the resonance moves. Measure the movement and you have measured the binding — continuously, without fluorescent labels [3, 4].

Three questions organise the rest of the article:

  1. What is the wave? A surface plasmon polariton: electrons in the metal oscillating together with an electromagnetic field, bound to the interface (sections 02–05).
  2. How does light launch it? The wave carries more momentum than light of the same frequency, so a coupler is needed: a prism, a grating, a waveguide or a fibre (sections 06–10 and 14–17).
  3. How is a change read out, and how well? By angle, wavelength, intensity or phase; with a sensitivity, a linewidth and a figure of merit that follow from the same physics (sections 11–13).

02Free electrons and a negative permittivity

Start with what makes a metal a metal: some of its electrons are not tied to atoms. Push them with an oscillating electric field \(E(t) = E_0 e^{-i\omega t}\) and each one obeys Newton's law with a friction term,

$$m\,\ddot{x} + m\gamma\,\dot{x} = -e\,E(t),$$

where \(m\) and \(-e\) are the electron's mass and charge, \(x\) its displacement and \(\gamma\) a damping rate (collisions, about \(10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\)). The solution \(x = eE/[m(\omega^2 + i\gamma\omega)]\) gives a polarisation \(P = -n_e e x\) for \(n_e\) electrons per unit volume, and therefore a relative permittivity [2, 6]

$$\varepsilon_m(\omega) = 1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2 + i\gamma\omega}, \qquad \omega_p^2 = \frac{n_e e^2}{\varepsilon_0 m}.$$

This is the Drude model. \(\omega\) is the angular frequency of the light, \(\varepsilon_0\) the permittivity of free space and \(\omega_p\) the plasma frequency — for gold and silver in the ultraviolet, near 9 eV.

What the equation says is easiest to see. A free electron has no spring holding it in place, so the oscillating push \(-eE\) only accelerates it, and an accelerated mass is displaced opposite to its acceleration: the electron's displacement lags the push by almost 180° (exactly 180° without damping). The electrons therefore end up displaced along \(E\), and since their charge is negative the dipole they form, \(P = -n_e e x\), points against \(E\). That makes a negative contribution, \(-\omega_p^2/\omega^2\), to the permittivity. At low frequency the electrons have plenty of time to move far and this contribution outweighs the vacuum's 1: below \(\omega_p\) the real part of \(\varepsilon_m\) becomes negative. In a slab with the field across it, the charge piled up at the two faces adds its own field, and the total field inside, \(E_\text{in} = E_0/\varepsilon_m\), is reversed and weaker than the field outside: the metal screens the light out and reflects it. Above \(\omega_p\) the electrons cannot keep up, \(0 < \varepsilon_m < 1\), and the metal becomes transparent (the alkali metals are transparent in the ultraviolet for this reason). Exactly at \(\omega_p\), \(\varepsilon_m \approx 0\) and the electron sea can oscillate on its own: the bulk plasmon.

Visible and near-infrared light sits far below \(\omega_p\), so for gold and silver \(\varepsilon_m' \) is strongly negative and grows more negative with wavelength, while the imaginary part \(\varepsilon_m''\) describes absorption. Real metals add interband transitions (in gold, below about 600 nm) that the free-electron model leaves out, so in this article the measured optical constants of Johnson and Christy [5] are used throughout.

A negative permittivity is the whole secret. In a dielectric the bound charges follow the field and partly screen it; in a metal the free electrons over-respond. At a boundary between a material with \(\varepsilon > 0\) and one with \(\varepsilon < 0\), the normal component of the field flips sign — and that, as the next section shows, is exactly what a surface wave needs.

03A wave that clings to a surface

Take a flat boundary at \(z = 0\): a dielectric with permittivity \(\varepsilon_d\) above (\(z > 0\)), a metal with \(\varepsilon_m\) below. Ask Maxwell's equations a precise question: is there a wave that travels along the surface, in \(x\), and decays away from it on both sides? Such a wave has the form \(e^{i(k_x x - \omega t)}\) times \(e^{-\kappa_d z}\) above and \(e^{+\kappa_m z}\) below, with both decay constants \(\kappa_d, \kappa_m\) positive. Each medium separately demands the wave equation, which links the three wavevector components:

$$\kappa_d^2 = k_x^2 - \varepsilon_d k_0^2, \qquad \kappa_m^2 = k_x^2 - \varepsilon_m k_0^2, \qquad k_0 = \frac{\omega}{c} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.$$

Here \(k_x\) is the wavevector along the surface, \(k_0\) the wavevector of light in vacuum, \(c\) the speed of light and \(\lambda\) the vacuum wavelength. What remains is to satisfy the boundary conditions — and here the two polarisations behave completely differently. Following the convention of Part 1, section 10, the plane of incidence contains the propagation direction \(x\) and the surface normal \(z\). In TM (p) polarisation the magnetic field \(\mathbf{H} = H_y \hat{y}\) points out of that plane and \(\mathbf{E}\) lies in it; in TE (s) polarisation it is the other way round.

The TM case

Write \(H_y = A\,e^{i k_x x}e^{-\kappa_d z}\) above and \(H_y = B\,e^{i k_x x}e^{\kappa_m z}\) below. Ampère's law, \(\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = -i\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\mathbf{E}\), gives the electric field from \(H_y\):

$$E_x = \frac{1}{i\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial z}, \qquad E_z = -\frac{1}{i\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{k_x}{\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon}\,H_y .$$

At the boundary the tangential components \(H_y\) and \(E_x\) must be continuous. The first gives \(A = B\). The second, with \(\partial H_y/\partial z = -\kappa_d H_y\) above and \(+\kappa_m H_y\) below, gives

$$\frac{\kappa_d}{\varepsilon_d} + \frac{\kappa_m}{\varepsilon_m} = 0 .$$

Both \(\kappa\)'s are positive (the wave must decay), so this can only hold if \(\varepsilon_d\) and \(\varepsilon_m\) have opposite signs — a dielectric against a metal. Squaring and substituting the two expressions for \(\kappa^2\) gives the dispersion relation of the surface plasmon polariton (SPP) [1, 2, 7]:

$$k_{sp} = k_0\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_m\,\varepsilon_d}{\varepsilon_m + \varepsilon_d}}, \qquad n_{sp} \equiv \frac{\operatorname{Re} k_{sp}}{k_0}.$$

For \(k_{sp}\) to be real and larger than the light's, the denominator must be negative: \(\varepsilon_m' < -\varepsilon_d\). That is the line drawn in Figure 2. With gold and water at 633 nm (\(\varepsilon_m \approx -11.8 + 1.2i\), \(\varepsilon_d = 1.774\)) one finds \(n_{sp} \approx 1.44\) — larger than water's index, 1.333. The number \(n_{sp}\) acts as an effective index of the surface wave: it travels along the surface as if through a medium of index 1.44. Because \(\varepsilon_m\) has an imaginary part, so does \(k_{sp}\): the wave is absorbed as it travels.

04Why only TM light excites a surface plasmon

Repeat the same calculation for TE polarisation. Now \(E_y = A\,e^{ik_x x}e^{-\kappa_d z}\) above and \(B\,e^{ik_x x}e^{\kappa_m z}\) below. Continuity of \(E_y\) gives \(A = B\). Faraday's law gives \(H_x \propto \partial E_y/\partial z\), and continuity of \(H_x\) (no magnetic response, \(\mu = 1\) on both sides) gives

$$A\,(\kappa_d + \kappa_m) = 0 .$$

Both \(\kappa\)'s are positive, so their sum cannot vanish: \(A = 0\). There is no TE surface wave on a metal — no matter what the metal's permittivity is, because the permittivity never entered this condition [1, 2].

The physical reason is just as clear. A surface plasmon is a wave of surface charge: bands of extra electrons and of missing electrons that travel along the interface, with field lines that leave the positive bands and return to the negative ones. Gauss's law ties the surface charge density \(\sigma\) to the jump in the normal component of the field: with no free charge the displacement field \(D_z = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon E_z\) is continuous, so \(E_z\) itself jumps, and the jump is the polarisation (bound) surface charge, \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0(E_{z,d} - E_{z,m})\). Because \(\varepsilon_m < 0\), \(E_{z,m}\) has the opposite sign to \(E_{z,d}\) and the jump is large. A field that is everywhere parallel to the surface — TE — has no normal component, pushes the electrons sideways along the surface, and never piles up charge. With nothing to hold on to, no bound wave forms; the TE light is simply reflected.

Three practical consequences follow and are worth remembering:

  • Every SPR instrument uses p-polarised light — TM with respect to the metal surface. In a prism coupler that means the electric field lies in the plane containing the beam and the surface normal.
  • s-polarised light is the perfect reference. It travels the same path but never couples; dividing the p signal by the s signal removes source drifts and detector effects. Figure 8 shows both.
  • On a grating, "TM" is defined relative to the grooves. The field must have a component along the grating vector \(\mathbf{K}\) (perpendicular to the grooves) and normal to the surface — see section 14 and Part 1, section 10 for how TE and TM are assigned on gratings.

05The plasmon in numbers: reach, travel and momentum

The dispersion relation fixes everything a sensor designer needs. Three lengths matter:

$$\delta_d = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Re}\kappa_d} = \frac{1}{k_0\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{n_{sp}^2 - \varepsilon_d}}, \qquad \delta_m = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Re}\kappa_m}, \qquad L_{sp} = \frac{1}{2\operatorname{Im}k_{sp}} .$$

\(\delta_d\) is the distance into the dielectric over which the field falls to \(1/e\) — the depth the sensor "sees"; \(\delta_m\) is the same inside the metal (a few tens of nanometres, which is why the metal film can be thin); and \(L_{sp}\) is the propagation length, over which the plasmon's intensity falls to \(1/e\) as it travels along the surface. In the square root, \(n_{sp}\) is complex here, so the formula uses the full \(k_{sp}/k_0\).

Two trade-offs are already visible. A longer wavelength gives a longer \(L_{sp}\) — a narrower resonance, because the width of any resonance is inversely proportional to the lifetime of what resonates — but also a deeper reach \(\delta_d\), so a thin layer of molecules fills a smaller fraction of the field (section 13). And silver beats gold on \(L_{sp}\) everywhere, at the cost of chemical stability.

06The momentum gap

Since \(\varepsilon_m' < -\varepsilon_d\), the fraction under the square root is larger than \(\varepsilon_d\):

$$n_{sp} > \sqrt{\varepsilon_d} = n_d .$$

Now look at light arriving from the dielectric at angle \(\theta\). Only the component of its wavevector along the surface, \(k_x = n_d k_0 \sin\theta\), can match the plasmon's (the boundary conditions hold at every \(x\) only if both fields vary as the same \(e^{ik_x x}\)). But \(\sin\theta \le 1\), so \(k_x \le n_d k_0 < \operatorname{Re}k_{sp}\). At every angle and every wavelength, light from the dielectric is short of momentum. On a dispersion diagram the plasmon curve always lies to the right of the light line; they never cross (Figure 5). That is why a gold mirror does not show plasmon dips in ordinary reflection [1].

Every SPR configuration is a way of supplying the missing in-plane momentum. Before looking at the hardware it helps to see the bookkeeping on a single axis: plot \(k_x/k_0\) (an effective index), mark \(n_{sp}\), and add up what each coupler contributes.

07Four ways to bridge the gap

There are four families of couplers [3, 4, 28]:

  • Prism coupling (attenuated total reflection). Light in glass of index \(n_p > n_d\) is totally reflected at the base of the prism; its evanescent field, with \(k_x = n_p k_0 \sin\theta\), tunnels to the metal–dielectric interface. In the Kretschmann geometry [10] the metal film sits on the prism; in the Otto geometry [9] a thin gap separates prism and metal.
  • Grating coupling. A periodic surface of period \(\Lambda\) adds \(m\cdot 2\pi/\Lambda\) to the in-plane wavevector of the diffracted light [1, 16].
  • Waveguide coupling. A mode guided in a planar waveguide already has an effective index above the cover's; where the waveguide is coated with metal, the mode hands energy to the plasmon [27].
  • Fibre coupling — waveguide coupling in an optical fibre: rays in the core strike a gold-coated, unclad section at grazing angles [17, 18].

08Prism coupling I: the Kretschmann configuration

In 1968 Kretschmann and Raether evaporated a thin silver film directly onto a prism and shone light through the glass [10]. At angles beyond the critical angle for glass–dielectric, \(\theta_c = \arcsin(n_d/n_p)\), the light is totally reflected at the glass–metal side, but its evanescent field penetrates the thin metal and reaches the outer surface with in-plane wavevector \(n_p k_0 \sin\theta\). At the angle where

$$n_p \sin\theta_\text{res} = n_{sp} = \operatorname{Re}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_m\,\varepsilon_d}{\varepsilon_m + \varepsilon_d}}$$

the plasmon on the outer surface (facing the analyte) is excited, energy flows into it and is absorbed in the metal, and the reflected beam darkens. The thin film is essential: it lets the light tunnel through, and its outer surface is where the sample is. Liedberg, Nylander and Lundström used exactly this geometry for the first SPR gas sensor and biosensor in 1983 [11].

The exact reflectance

For three media — prism (\(p\)), metal film (\(m\), thickness \(d\)), dielectric (\(d\)) — the reflection coefficient follows from summing the multiply reflected waves in the film (the Airy sum) [12, 13]:

$$r = \frac{r_{pm} + r_{md}\,e^{2ik_{z,m}d}}{1 + r_{pm}\,r_{md}\,e^{2ik_{z,m}d}}, \qquad r_{ij} = \frac{k_{z,i}/\varepsilon_i - k_{z,j}/\varepsilon_j}{k_{z,i}/\varepsilon_i + k_{z,j}/\varepsilon_j}, \qquad k_{z,j} = k_0\sqrt{\varepsilon_j - n_p^2\sin^2\theta} .$$

\(r_{ij}\) is the Fresnel coefficient for p-polarisation at the interface between media \(i\) and \(j\) (written with the \(k_z/\varepsilon\) "admittances" of TM waves), \(k_{z,j}\) the wavevector component normal to the layers in medium \(j\), and \(R = |r|^2\) the reflectance. For s-polarisation the same formula holds with \(k_{z,i}\) in place of \(k_{z,i}/\varepsilon_i\). For more layers (an adhesion layer, a bound film) the transfer-matrix method chains the same algebra [13]; every reflectance curve in this article comes from it.

What the dip's shape means

Near the resonance the exact formula reduces to a Lorentzian [1]:

$$R \approx 1 - \frac{4\,\Gamma_i\,\Gamma_\text{rad}}{\left(k_x - k^0_{sp}\right)^2 + \left(\Gamma_i + \Gamma_\text{rad}\right)^2}, \qquad k_x = n_p k_0\sin\theta .$$

\(k^0_{sp}\) is the real part of the plasmon wavevector (slightly shifted by the prism's presence), \(\Gamma_i = \operatorname{Im}k_{sp}\) the internal damping (absorption in the metal), and \(\Gamma_\text{rad}\) the radiative damping — the rate at which the plasmon leaks back through the film into the prism, which falls off as \(e^{-2\operatorname{Re}\kappa_m d}\) with film thickness. Two conclusions follow directly:

  • The dip reaches zero when \(\Gamma_\text{rad} = \Gamma_i\) — critical coupling, just as for any resonator fed through a partly transparent mirror.
  • Its full width in \(k_x\) is \(2(\Gamma_i + \Gamma_\text{rad})\); at critical coupling \(4\,\Gamma_i\). Converted to angle, \(\Delta\theta \approx 4\operatorname{Im}n_{sp}/(n_p\cos\theta_\text{res})\) radians. The width is set by the metal's loss.

09Choosing the metal film

Thickness. Thin films radiate strongly (\(\Gamma_\text{rad} > \Gamma_i\)): the dip is broad and shallow. Thick films barely let the light through (\(\Gamma_\text{rad} < \Gamma_i\)): narrow but shallow again. In between lies critical coupling — about 50 nm for gold across the red and near-infrared, and somewhat more (55–60 nm) for silver (Figure 9).

Gold or silver. The width scales with \(\operatorname{Im}n_{sp}\), which is set by the loss ratio \(\varepsilon_m''/|\varepsilon_m'|\) (Figure 2). Silver's ratio is several times smaller, so its dip is several times sharper. But silver tarnishes in air and dissolves in many buffers, so sensor chips almost always use gold — or silver protected by a few nanometres of gold (the "Silver + 10 nm gold cap" option in Figure 8), which keeps most of silver's sharpness with gold's surface chemistry.

Adhesion layers. Gold sticks poorly to glass, so a 1–5 nm layer of chromium or titanium is evaporated first (the practicalities are in Part 8). These metals are much lossier than gold. In the red, where gold's own loss already sets a broad dip, a few nanometres change little; in the near-infrared, where the gold dip is sharp, 5 nm of chromium broadens it by well over half (set Figure 8 to 850 nm and try it). The adhesion layer also adds to the total metal thickness, so the gold must be thinned to stay at critical coupling. Keeping the adhesion layer as thin as possible, or replacing it with a molecular adhesion promoter, is part of good chip design.

Wavelength. Moving from 633 nm to 850 nm narrows the gold dip several-fold (compare in Figure 8), because gold's loss ratio falls and the interband absorption is left behind. The price is a deeper reach into the liquid (section 13).

10Prism coupling II: the Otto configuration

Otto's arrangement, published the same year, turns the geometry around [9]: the prism faces the metal across a thin gap of a dielectric (air, or the analyte), and the metal can be as thick as one likes. Light totally reflected at the prism's base sends its evanescent field across the gap to the metal surface, where it excites the plasmon at the same matching condition \(n_p\sin\theta = n_{sp}\) — here with \(n_{sp}\) computed for the gap medium.

The gap plays the role that the film thickness plays in Kretschmann's geometry: it sets the radiative damping. Too narrow, and the prism over-couples and broadens the dip; too wide, and the evanescent field never reaches the metal. The best gap is of the order of the wavelength (Figure 10). Holding a gap of a few hundred nanometres uniform over a millimetre-sized spot is awkward, which is why Otto's geometry is rarely used in biosensing. It shines where the metal cannot be a thin film — single crystals, bulk samples, or surfaces that must not be touched from behind.

11Reading the resonance: angle, wavelength, intensity, phase

The resonance condition couples two variables, angle and wavelength. Fix one and scan the other, or fix both and watch something else [3, 4]:

  • Angular interrogation. Monochromatic light (a laser diode or LED) arrives as a converging fan of angles; a detector array records \(R(\theta)\), and software tracks the dip's centre. Robust and widely used; the readout is in degrees or millidegrees.
  • Wavelength (spectral) interrogation. White light at one fixed angle; a spectrometer records \(R(\lambda)\). No moving parts and natural for fibres; the readout is in nanometres.
  • Intensity interrogation. Fixed angle and wavelength on the steep flank of the dip; the reflected power changes in proportion to the index change. Simple optics, but linear only over a small range and sensitive to source drift.
  • Phase interrogation. Near critical coupling, the phase of \(r_p\) jumps steeply across the resonance — far more steeply than the intensity changes. Interferometric or polarimetric set-ups compare the p-phase with the s-phase and reach very low detection limits [21].

12Sensitivity, width and figure of merit

Angular sensitivity

Differentiate the matching condition \(n_p\sin\theta_\text{res} = n_{sp}(n_d)\) with respect to the analyte index \(n_d\). Using \(n_{sp}^2 = \varepsilon_m n_d^2/(\varepsilon_m + n_d^2)\) with \(\varepsilon_m\) taken as real for the moment, a few lines of algebra give \(\mathrm{d}n_{sp}/\mathrm{d}n_d = (n_{sp}/n_d)^3\), and so

$$S_\theta \equiv \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_\text{res}}{\mathrm{d}n_d} = \frac{(n_{sp}/n_d)^3}{n_p\cos\theta_\text{res}}\quad(\text{radians per refractive-index unit, RIU}).$$

For gold, water and BK7 at 633 nm this gives about 150°/RIU, close to the exact 147°/RIU of Figure 8. A lower-index prism pushes the resonance to larger angles, where \(\cos\theta\) is small: the sensitivity rises (try SF10 or SF11 against BK7 in Figure 8: about 77°/RIU and 70°/RIU against 147°/RIU). The dip narrows in the same proportion, so the figure of merit hardly depends on the prism (compare the prisms in Figure 12).

Spectral sensitivity

At fixed angle, the resonance wavelength moves by

$$S_\lambda \equiv \frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda_\text{res}}{\mathrm{d}n_d} = \frac{(n_{sp}/n_d)^3}{\left|\dfrac{\mathrm{d}n_{sp}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} - \sin\theta\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}n_p}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\right|} ,$$

where the denominator is how fast the mismatch between plasmon and prism changes with wavelength. In the near-infrared \(n_{sp}\) flattens towards \(n_d\) (the plasmon becomes more light-like), the denominator becomes tiny and \(S_\lambda\) climbs to many thousands of nm/RIU (Figure 12).

Width and figure of merit

A big shift is useless if the dip is broad: what matters is how precisely the dip's centre can be located. The figure of merit

$$\text{FOM} = \frac{S}{\text{FWHM}}\quad(\text{RIU}^{-1})$$

compares the shift with the full width at half maximum, both in the same units (degrees or nm). Because sensitivity and width are both governed by the same dispersion relation, the FOM is largely independent of whether the dip is read in angle or wavelength. It grows with wavelength and is several times larger for silver than for gold. The final resolution — the smallest index change distinguishable from noise — depends on the FOM, the noise of the light source and detector, the number of points fitted and temperature control (water's index changes by about \(10^{-4}\) per kelvin) [22].

13Bulk versus surface sensitivity

The numbers above are bulk sensitivities: the whole dielectric changes index. In biosensing only a thin layer changes — a few nanometres of protein at the surface. Since the plasmon's intensity falls as \(e^{-2z/\delta_d}\), a layer of thickness \(t\) and index \(n_a\) on a buffer of index \(n_b\) acts roughly like a bulk change scaled by the fraction of the field's energy it occupies:

$$\Delta\theta \approx S_\theta\,(n_a - n_b)\left(1 - e^{-2t/\delta_d}\right) \approx S_\theta\,(n_a - n_b)\,\frac{2t}{\delta_d}\quad(t \ll \delta_d).$$

So for thin layers what matters is not \(S_\theta\) alone but \(S_\theta/\delta_d\). A short reach concentrates the field where the molecules are. This is why most biosensors work in the red (633–700 nm) rather than deep in the infrared, even though the infrared gives larger bulk sensitivity and a sharper dip; and why large targets (cells, vesicles, bacteria) prefer the longer reach of the infrared or of long-range plasmons (section 17).

Turning this surface sensitivity into a biosensor — choosing the capture layer, its thickness, its density and orientation — is the subject of the next part, Putting antibodies on an SPR sensor.

14Grating coupling

Wood noticed in 1902 that the spectrum diffracted by a metal grating showed sudden dark bands for p-polarised light [14]; Fano explained them in 1941 as surface waves on the metal [15]. A grating of period \(\Lambda\) can diffract light into orders whose in-plane wavevector is increased by multiples of the grating vector \(K = 2\pi/\Lambda\). When one of those orders matches the plasmon,

$$n_d\sin\theta + m\,\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda} = \pm\, n_{sp}(\lambda), \qquad m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots ,$$

energy goes into the plasmon and the specularly reflected (zeroth-order) light dips. Here \(\theta\) is the angle of incidence measured in the analyte (the light comes through the sample, not through a prism), \(m\) the diffraction order and the ± sign the direction in which the plasmon runs. At normal incidence and first order the condition is simply \(\lambda_\text{res} \approx \Lambda\,n_{sp}\): the resonance follows the period. Cullen, Brown and Lowe used gold-coated diffraction gratings to detect immunocomplex formation in the late 1980s [16].

Two branches appear when the angle is tilted (\(m = +1\) and \(m = -1\)), and they cross at normal incidence, where the two counter-propagating plasmons interact and open a small band gap (not captured by the simple matching condition). The groove depth sets the radiative damping: shallow grooves couple weakly (narrow, shallow dip), deep grooves over-couple — the same critical-coupling story as the film thickness in Kretschmann's geometry [1].

Polarisation on a grating. The plasmon is driven by the field component along the grating vector, perpendicular to the grooves. In the usual "classical" mounting — grooves perpendicular to the plane of incidence — that means TM (p) light, as on a flat surface. In a "conical" mounting the grooves are rotated and both s and p have a component along \(\mathbf{K}\); the bookkeeping is in Part 1, section 10.

Grating sensors are cheap to mass-produce by moulding or embossing, need no prism and no index-matching oil, and can be read from the sample side. Their spectral sensitivity, roughly \(\Lambda(n_{sp}/n_d)^3/m\) near normal incidence, is usually several times lower than a prism's in the near-infrared (compare Figures 11 and 13), and the light must pass through the sample, so absorbing or turbid samples and flow cells need care [3, 4].

15Waveguide coupling

A planar dielectric waveguide carries light in modes whose effective index \(n_\text{eff}\) lies between the indices of the cladding and the core (Part 1, section 14). Coat a short section with gold and the guided mode's evanescent tail reaches the metal. If \(n_\text{eff}\) of the mode equals \(n_{sp}\) of the plasmon on the outer surface — the waveguide analogue of the matching condition — power transfers from the mode to the plasmon and is absorbed; the transmitted light shows a dip in its spectrum [27].

The phase matching is set by the waveguide's design (its index and thickness) rather than by an external angle, so waveguide SPR sensors are usually read spectrally. They are compact, robust and well suited to integration — several sensing sections on one chip, light sources and detectors on the same substrate [3, 4, 27]. Their practical limitation is that only one angle is available (the mode's), so the operating wavelength must be designed in, and the coupling is strongly polarisation-dependent: only the TM-like mode of the guide couples.

16Fibre-optic SPR

An optical fibre is a waveguide too, and fibre SPR sensors — first demonstrated by Jorgenson and Yee in 1993 [17] — are the most widespread waveguide-coupled sensors. In the simplest probe a few millimetres to centimetres of a multimode fibre are stripped of cladding and coated with about 50 nm of gold; white light is launched at one end and a spectrometer records what arrives at the other. In the ray picture, a guided ray travelling at an angle \(\alpha\) to the fibre axis strikes the gold at \(\theta = 90^\circ - \alpha\) — grazing incidence, ≳ 70° — and is reflected \(N(\alpha) = L\tan\alpha/D\) times in a sensing length \(L\) of a core of diameter \(D\). Every reflection is a Kretschmann reflection with the core playing the prism [18]:

$$T(\lambda) = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{\alpha_\text{max}} p(\alpha)\,\tfrac12\left[R_p(\theta,\lambda)^{N(\alpha)} + R_s(\theta,\lambda)^{N(\alpha)}\right]\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\displaystyle\int_0^{\alpha_\text{max}} p(\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha}, \qquad \sin\alpha_\text{max} = \frac{\mathrm{NA}}{n_\text{core}} .$$

\(p(\alpha)\) is the power carried by rays at angle \(\alpha\) — here a uniformly filled numerical aperture, \(p \propto \sin\alpha\cos\alpha\), for meridional rays — and NA the fibre's numerical aperture. In a multimode fibre light of both polarisations travels in every direction, so p and s are averaged: only half the light can couple, and the dip never reaches zero. Each ray angle resonates at a different wavelength, and the spectrum is a superposition of all of them; that is why fibre SPR dips are broader than prism dips.

Three design choices stand out in the model, and they match what is found in practice [18, 19]:

  • Sensing length and core diameter set the number of reflections. More reflections deepen the dip, but once the resonant rays are fully absorbed further length only broadens it.
  • Numerical aperture sets the range of angles, and with it the spread of resonance wavelengths: a smaller NA gives a narrower dip but needs a longer section.
  • Spectral sensitivity is high — about 2000 nm/RIU in water in this model, rising with the analyte's index — because the rays meet the gold near grazing incidence, where the matching wavelength changes quickly with the analyte's index.

Probe designs

17Long-range SPR and other refinements

A very thin metal film between two dielectrics of nearly equal index carries two coupled plasmons, one on each face. They combine into a symmetric and an antisymmetric mode; in the one that keeps most of its field outside the metal, the absorption drops dramatically and the plasmon travels much further — a long-range surface plasmon [8, 20]. In a Kretschmann-type set-up the prism is first covered with one to a few micrometres of a low-index buffer that matches the water's index, then with 10–25 nm of gold. The result is a dip that is many times narrower than the conventional one, with a field that reaches much further into the sample.

Other refinements follow the same physics: bimetallic films (silver for sharpness, gold for chemistry), high-index dielectric overlayers that pull the field to the surface, and metals other than gold and silver for other spectral ranges (aluminium in the ultraviolet) [4]. Each one changes either \(n_{sp}\) (and so the sensitivity) or \(\operatorname{Im}n_{sp}\) and \(\Gamma_\text{rad}\) (and so the width), and can be judged by its figure of merit and reach.
